Module 1 · Introduction to Production & Operations Management

Production & Operations Management · Notes

NotesCheat sheetMind map

Read this first: The Ebook covers the definition, 3 schools of thought, production as the 6 Ms transformation, planning-organising-controlling, scope, interfaces with other functions, automation, goods vs services, Porter's 3 generic strategies and the productivity family (labour, multifactor, total factor, wastivity). The Study Notes (which the LMS quizzes follow) come in 3 parts: (1) production fundamentals, objectives, 9 functions, relationship with other functions, fixed / programmable / flexible automation; (2) service vs manufacturing; (3) competitiveness, cost leadership, differentiation, flexibility, globalisation, productivity formula, benchmarking, KPIs, Lean, Six Sigma.

1. Definitions

  • Operations Management = management of an organisation's productive resources or its production system, which converts inputs into the organisation's products and services (Ebook).

  • Production = conversion of inputs, the 6 Ms (men, machines, materials, money, methods, management), into output through a transformation process. Output = goods (manufacturing) or services rendered (banks, hospitals, hotels). In this sense production management = operations management.

  • Agriculture was the first production activity (Ebook).

  • SN definition: "the methodical transformation of one type of material into another to improve or create the product for the user". Production = process of adding value = a transformational process.

  • Production is a primary business function with marketing and finance. Marketing creates demand, finance provides capital, production makes the goods. It employs most staff, holds most assets and is called the heart of the organisation.

Three schools of thought (Ebook)

School Emphasises
Classical (1) Economic efficiency = scientific management; (2) management as a continuous process of planning, organising, controlling = process management
Behavioural (1) Human relationship: subordinate-supervisor relationship affects productivity; (2) Behavioural science: leadership, motivation, communication, attitude
Modelling Decision-making · system management · mathematical modelling

QB traps: The branch that stresses mathematics = Modelling. "Behavioural management believes management is a continuous process" = False (that is Classical / process management).

Three ways to transform (SN)

Way Meaning Example
Disintegration One raw material → many outputs Steel → spoons, knives, plates
Assembling Many inputs → one output Assembly of a product
Service Adds value to the product TV after-sale service
  • Production function gives an edge through: more inventory turns, greater flexibility, higher efficiency, better quality, better customer service, no delays in delivery, reduced wastage (SN).

2. Objectives and importance

  • Ebook: major objective = produce quality goods and services; increase profitability through higher efficiency and productivity, quality, right price and right time. Achieved by optimal use of resources, minimising wastage, SQC for quality at minimal cost, quantitative decision techniques.

  • SN: customer satisfaction rests on right quality, right quantity, right time, right price. Primary objective = right quality, right quantity, minimum cost, per time schedule.

SN objective Key point
Right quality Set by consumer needs; best quality is not always the appropriate quality
Right quantity Over-production blocks capital in inventory; under-production causes scarcity
Right time Delivery timeliness = key measure of the production department
Right manufacturing cost Cost is fixed before production; produce at that cost
Minimise resource use Use inputs fully for minimum cost, quality, time
Minimise total cost Cut non-value-added activities; raise labour productivity
Maximise utilisation Use manpower and other resources optimally
  • Importance (SN, 5): accomplishment of objectives · goodwill · helps introduce new products (R&D) · supports other functional areas (marketing, finance, HR) · helps face competition.

3. Functions and scope

Ebook: management process

Function Includes
Planning Activities that set a course of action: product planning, facility planning, design of conversion process
Organising Structure of tasks and authority; assign authority and responsibility
Controlling Actual performance in line with planned; standards and communication networks

Ebook: scope (2 groups)

  • Production system design (production engineering): design of tools and drawings, equipment, size of firm, overall plans, location plans, plant layouts, materials handling; also human factors and R&D.

  • Analysis and control of production: Production planning = short-term production schedules, records of raw, finished and semi-finished stock, how resources will be used for predicted demand (QB answer). Production control = regulate work assignment, progress, remove discrepancies through control on inventory, on work in progress (production control) and on quality (process control).

SN: 9 functions and responsibilities

  1. Selection of product and design (fundamental duty).

  2. Selection of production process (technology, machines, material handling).

  3. Right production capacity (short and long term).

  4. Production planning: first step; routing = path and sequence of operations; scheduling = duration / timing of the task.

  5. Production control.

  6. Location planning: long-term strategic decision; affects cost, selling price, demand.

  7. Inventory control: track products from receipt to final destination.

  8. Work study = method study (motion study) + work measurement (time study).

  9. Maintenance and replacement of machines.

4. Production and other functions

Interface Ebook point
Marketing Marketing = gatekeeper of information; strategic positioning and segmentation drive manufacturing strategy. Conflicts: width of product line, delivery time, service or quality levels
Finance Capital equipment, cost control, price-volume decisions, inventories; finance supplies cost data; planning and budgeting depend on co-operation
Design Shrinking life cycles, high clock-speed; simultaneous engineering, early supplier involvement; process engineering + design + production
Human resource Continuous improvement and total quality rely on people; flextime; in services, customer contact staff form the customer's view
Information systems ERP, CIM, knowledge sharing, e-commerce

  • SN figure: manufacturing, personnel, product development, marketing, finance and accounting, purchasing around the policy-making group at the centre. Purchasing checks vendor credibility and coordinates purchase volume with requirements.

  • Distinctive competencies = strengths unique to the company: skilled workforce, distribution network, fast product development, quick change in output rates.

Production vs operations management (Ebook)

  • Operations management: ongoing management of daily work, no set end point (e.g. support of a deployed web project).

  • Production / product management (Ebook wording): planning, organising, staffing, leading, controlling for a specific function within constraints of time, cost, quality, with a completion date, like managing a project.

Misconception (LMS): Production management is not only about physical products; it covers services too. It is not only the production department's job; it works with marketing, finance, personnel and product development.

5. Automation

  • Ebook: self-controlling operation of machinery that reduces or dispenses with human control in normal conditions. First introduced in the late 1940s by the Ford Motor Company (QB answer).

  • SN: technology using computer-based, mechanical and electronic systems to operate and control production. Transfer lines have existed since roughly 1924.

Advantages (Ebook) Disadvantages (Ebook)
Replaces humans in tedious tasks Technology limits: cannot automate everything
Replaces humans in dangerous environments (fire, space, nuclear, under water) Cost is hard to predict; R&D may show no economic gain
Tasks beyond human capability (heavy, hot, cold, too fast or slow) High initial cost
Economy improvement

Three types of automated production systems (SN)

Type Key features Examples
Fixed Sequence fixed by equipment configuration; simple motions integrated in one piece of equipment; long runs, millions of units Automated assembly machines, transfer lines
Programmable Sequence changed by a program; products made in batches; set-up and reprogramming between batches (time-consuming) NC machine tools, industrial robots, PLCs
Flexible Extension of programmable; wide range of parts with almost no downtime; no batches needed; "soft variety" Different schedules and mixes of parts

Misconception (LMS): Flexible automation minimises downtime between styles; it does not eliminate it completely. Automation does not necessarily cause job losses; it creates jobs in programming, maintenance and optimisation.

  • Automated manufacturing systems: automated machine tools, transfer lines, automated assembly, industrial robots, automated material handling and storage, automated QC inspection.

  • CIM (computer-integrated manufacturing): computers used to design goods, schedule production, manage operations and other business tasks, integrated into one company-wide system (SN calls it "integrated manufacturing").

  • Case (Ebook): Toyota Kirloskar, Bidadi plant (60,000 vehicles, one of Toyota's least automated) plans higher automation in its second plant (1 lakh units); used the downturn to multi-skill workers; kaizen = continuous improvement.

6. Service vs manufacturing

Point Manufacturing Services
Output Tangible goods; made in one place, bought in another Intangible; consumed as created
Customer contact Indirect: research, design, sales, after-sales Direct, dynamic; co-creation (doctor-patient)
Inventory Can be stored; economies of scale Cannot store output; real-time capacity management; supporting inventory only (drugs in hospital, cheque books in bank)
Perishability n/a Unused slot is lost (consultant appointment, airline seat) → yield management (airlines, hotels)
Planning Forecast-driven Demand-driven, flexible
Quality metrics Specifications, defect rate, durability Customer satisfaction, timeliness, personalisation, service recovery
Strategy Lean manufacturing, FMS, modular production, smart inventory (JIT) Agile processes, dynamic workforce, customer-centric approach, digital platforms, AI, remote delivery

  • Ebook: services need more attention and planning than manufacturing; a manufacturing defect can be reworked before dispatch, a service happens in front of the customer. Customer contact is a key characteristic of services.

  • Manufacturers increasingly think of output as a service bundle (Mercedes remote diagnosis example).

  • Competitive strategies in services (SN): differentiate through superior customer service; build advantage through unique service offerings.

  • Productivity assessment in manufacturing (SN): output per input unit; e.g. 1,000 units ÷ (labour + materials + energy).

7. Competitiveness and strategy

Ebook: Porter's generic strategies

  • Two schools: Generic strategies (Michael Porter, industry-focused, emphasised) vs Resource-based approach (Prahalad and Hamel).

  • Two kinds of competitive advantage: low cost or differentiation. With scope these give three strategies:

Strategy Meaning
Cost leadership Economic costs below rivals → high margins, superior ROI
Differentiation Unique on dimensions buyers value → higher revenue / margins
Focus Narrow segment, buyer group or region; variants cost focus and differentiation focus

  • Sun-Tzu (Art of War): "The more opportunities that I seize, the more opportunities that multiply before me."

  • Hierarchy: corporate strategy (which business, resource deployment) → business / competitive strategy (how to compete in a market).

SN: competitiveness in operations

  • Competitiveness = relentless pursuit of efficiency, quality and innovation.

  • Operational excellence: 3 pillars efficiency, quality, innovation; goal = competitive edge through superior operational performance (more than meeting basic production needs).

  • Customer focus: align operations with customers' changing needs; efficient processes raise satisfaction.

SN strategy Core idea
Cost leadership Cost efficiency + economies of scale; not just lowest price but value (misconception)
Differentiation Innovation, design, quality → brand identity; more than product features
Flexibility and responsiveness Adjust to market changes; react quickly to customer needs
Globalisation Market reach + efficiency; manage international supply chains, culture, regulations; not only sales

8. Productivity

  • Productivity = Output ÷ Input. Conceptually, "an attitude of mind" and prevention of all kinds of waste. Higher productivity = more efficient system.

  • Green Productivity (GP): productivity growth while protecting the environment; launched by the Asian Productivity Organization (APO), Tokyo in 1994 after the Rio Earth Summit 1992 (QB: socio-economic view = Green productivity).

  • National level: rupee value of output per unit of labour; productivity matters more than money because it determines output.

Measure Meaning (Ebook)
Labour productivity Single factor measure: output per unit of labour per unit time (QB: single)
Multiple factor Output ÷ (labour + capital + materials + energy + other)
Partial productivity Output ÷ one class of input, e.g. value of output ÷ man-hours
Total factor productivity Year-by-year change in output considering many factors (education, R&D, labour relations, attitudes); more accurate than labour productivity

  • Productivity indices (Ebook): OMS = output per man-shift (coal miner 2 t/day); steel plant 10,000 t per month; GNP: national productivity given as per capita income; agriculture: output per hectare; incentive schemes: SMH ÷ AMH (standard man-hours earned ÷ actual man-hours worked).

  • Wastivity = 1 ÷ Productivity; waste = idle resources (inventory waiting, patients waiting), defective goods. Cutting scrap by 1% can save as much as raising production and sales by 10-15%.

Worked example (Ebook)

  • Standard time 5 h/unit, 126 units, 4 persons, allowed delays 44 man-hours. Earned standard hours = 5 × 126 = 630. Available = 4 × 25 × 8 = 800 − 44 = 756. Productivity = 630 ÷ 756 = 83.33%; wastivity = 16.67%.

Raising productivity

  • Three ways: more output with same input; less input for same output; output up faster than input.

  • Objectives of improvement: efficiency, maximum output, economy, quality, elimination of waste, satisfaction of human beings (QB: satisfaction of human resource improves productivity).

  • Waste elimination through: technology, innovation and automation · learning and experience (first seen in the aircraft industry) · job design, work analysis and motivation (Hawthorne studies, Elton Mayo: better lighting raised output at no cost).

  • Productivity paradox (Robert Solow): "computers everywhere except in the productivity statistics"; service productivity lags manufacturing. Network effect may turn it into a boom.

  • Labour productivity can mislead: outsourcing and lay-offs raise it while sales stay the same.

  • Productivity tree: roots = inputs, trunk = conversion, fruit = outputs.

SN: tools to measure and improve

Tool Key point
Benchmarking Compare performance with industry standards and peers; find gaps, root causes, adopt best practices; ongoing
Performance metrics (KPIs) Aligned with strategy, quantifiable, real-time, cover quality, lead time, inventory turnover, customer satisfaction
Lean Eliminate waste (overproduction, excess inventory, motion, defects); Kaizen = "change for the better"
Six Sigma Reduce process variation statistically; DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control
PDCA Plan-Do-Check-Act = Deming cycle

SN KC answers: Productivity formula is not complex (False) · regular productivity assessment is not mainly short-term (False) · benchmarking compares performance (True) · KPIs drive continuous improvement (True).

9. Case: TI Cycles vs Hero Cycles (Ebook)

  • TI Cycles (Murugappa group, Sept 1949, with Hercules Cycles UK): elegant BSA and Hercules bikes; vertically integrated (made its own steel tubes).

  • Hero Cycles (Munjal brothers, Ludhiana): heavy-duty, low-cost bikes for Indian needs; outsourced components to ancillaries; used JIT early; undercut TI by 15-20%; launched Hero Ranger (MTB) 1989, Allegro.

  • TI failed through customer disconnect and missing disruptive forces. 1994-95: loss ₹2.98 cr on ₹208.28 cr sales, slipped to No. 3. Then TQM (Jan 1998), ISO 9000 (March 2000); productivity rose from 2.45 to 5.78 cycles per man per day.

  • Lesson: mission answers "Who are we?" and "What do we want to be?"; operations capability must match the strategic choice.
