1. Quality control defined

  • "Quality is not by chance but by intention" (German cars, Swiss watches, Japanese electronics).

  • Quality Control = working to a set standard of quality which is achievable and has a ready market; adherence to the standard, i.e. preventing change.

Control Breakthrough
Preventing change: hold the existing standard Creating change: raise the standard (what customers accept today may not be accepted next year)
Most managers are stuck here with daily problems Fails if affected people are not involved; training helps change attitudes
  • Objective of QC: coordinate quality maintenance and improvement by all groups for full consumer satisfaction. SQC gets there most economically (less scrap, rework, downtime, inspection): "better quality to the consumer at a lower cost".
Quality of design Quality of performance
What the target is: Ambassador vs Maruti differ in design How well the target is hit
SQC sometimes improves design too SQC is mainly concerned with this
  • Quick Notes: quality assurance drives customer satisfaction, trust and loyalty, and competitiveness. Misconception: QA is not only about meeting standards.

2. Inspection

  • Inspection is an important tool to achieve quality concept: assures the maker, satisfies the customer, controls quality, cuts cost and scrap, removes assignable causes. Inspection and test unit appraises incoming material, components and output.

  • Variables: inspection measures degree of conformance. Attributes: it only separates conforming from non-conforming. Inspection does not show why defects occur. It is a function of quality control.

5 objectives of inspection 9 purposes of inspection
Remove faulty raw material before production Good lots vs bad lots · good pieces vs bad pieces
Detect faulty products during production Is the process changing? Nearing specification limits?
Alert managers before problems get serious Rate product quality · rate inspector accuracy
Stop substandard goods reaching customers Measure instrument precision
Build reputation for quality and reliability Secure design information · measure process capability

7 types of inspection

Type Key point
Floor At the place of production by patrolling inspectors (highly skilled); less handling, quick correction
Centralised Central place with all test equipment; sensitive kit in air-conditioned area; samples brought in
Combined Mix of floor and central; locate and prevent defects economically
Functional Checks only the main function (motor speed and load); does not reveal part variation; also called assembly inspection
First piece / first-off First piece of shift or lot; used with automatic machines
Pilot piece Done immediately after a new design/product is developed; shop floor or pilot plant; autos, aeroplanes
Final After work is complete; process industries (electroplating, anodizing); with incoming inspection

Methods: 100% vs sampling

100% inspection Sampling inspection
Every piece, non-destructive tests Random representative samples
Costly, many inspectors Cheaper, quicker, fewer inspectors
No sampling error, but inspector fatigue/negligence errors Sampling error, but it can be estimated
Small numbers or very high quality: jet engines, aircraft, medical equipment Destructive tests; low-cost items: bulbs, washing machines

Clinic: Every coronary stent lot gets 100%-style checks because failure is catastrophic (like jet engines). Gloves and gauze get sampling inspection, like bulbs.

3. Statistical process control (SPC)

  • SPC = applying statistical techniques to check whether process output conforms to design. Aims at quality through prevention rather than detection: control the process and the product will be good.

  • Quick Notes: SPC tools = control charts and process capability analysis; charts separate common from special causes.

Control charts

  • SPC is implemented through control charts. They monitor processes whose output is measured as variables or attributes.
Variable charts Attribute charts
Quality characteristic can be measured Cannot be measured; visual good/bad, accept/reject; count of non-conforming items
X-bar, R-bar, sigma charts p, np, c, u charts

  • A control chart is a time-ordered diagram with: centre line (nominal value = average of past samples) · UCL (Upper Control Limit) and LCL (Lower Control Limit) · data points (sample averages). By the Central Limit Theorem, sample means follow a normal distribution whatever the underlying distribution.

  • Benefits: flags when something is wrong · patterns suggest causes and remedies · estimates process capability · guides improvement.

  • Objectives (6): info to set/change specifications · production procedures · inspection/acceptance procedures · basis for current production decisions · basis for accept/reject decisions · familiarise staff with charts.

Clinic: An INR chart for a warfarin clinic is a control chart: target line in the middle, upper and lower limits, a dot per visit. A run of dots drifting up is a special cause worth chasing before a bleed.

4. Quality circles

  • Began in Japan in the 1960s. Based on participative management: morale and motivation through informal group discussion. Informal group of operators, supervisors, managers.

  • Juran: "a group of work force level people, usually from within one department, who volunteer to meet weekly (on company time) to address quality problems in their department."

  • Members select the problems and are trained in problem-solving techniques. Benefits: measurable savings + better attitudes. Two problem types: worker well-being and company well-being.

Effect on Benefits
Individual Builds capability, self-respect; shy people become active
Relations with others Supervisor and management respect workers; workers understand supervisors' difficulties
Workers and attitudes Change negative attitudes; less conflict; understand why problems get solved
  • Principles: people want to do a good job · be recognised and participate · have information · get recognition, responsibility, self-esteem. Motivation alone is not enough: need management support, technical knowledge, statistical procedures.

5. Acceptance sampling

  • Objective: decide whether to accept or reject a lot based on the sample's characteristics. Lot = incoming raw material or finished parts. Works for attributes and variables.

  • Why not 100%: high cost · destructive tests spoil everything · too slow · impossible for large/infinite populations.

  • Lot size N, sample n. Defectives follow binomial distribution when the lot is very large/infinite; otherwise hypergeometric.

Single sampling plan: n = 50, acceptance number c = 3. Draw 50; if defectives ≤ 3 accept the whole lot, otherwise reject it.

Risk What happens Symbol
Producer's risk = Type I error Good lot rejected; supplier penalised α
Consumer's risk = Type II error Bad lot accepted; customer loses β

  • OC curve: x-axis = % defective (lot quality); y-axis = probability of accepting. AQL (acceptable quality level): at or below it customers are satisfied; acceptance probability = 1 − α. LTPD (lot tolerance % defective): at or above it lot is inferior; acceptance probability = β. Between AQL and LTPD = indifferent zone.

  • Designing a single plan needs α, β, AQL, LTPD; find integer n and c so the OC curve passes (approximately) through (AQL, 1 − α) and (LTPD, β).

  • Quick Notes: sampling methods = simple random, stratified, systematic. Misconception: acceptance sampling assesses batch quality but does not guarantee it.

Clinic: Type I = calling a healthy patient diseased (false positive; producer's risk). Type II = missing real disease (false negative; consumer's risk). Same α and β you know from trial design.

6. Quick Notes only: cost of quality, ISO, TQC, KAIZEN, Six Sigma

Cost of quality Meaning
Prevention Stop defects before they occur (training, design, planning)
Appraisal Assess quality: inspection, testing
Failure: internal / external Defects found; external = after reaching the customer

  • ISO 9000: family of international quality management system standards by the International Organization for Standardization; aim = customer satisfaction. Components: quality policy and objectives · process approach · risk-based thinking. ISO 14000 = environmental management. Misconception: ISO 9000 is not about environment.

  • TQC (Total Quality Control): holistic, organisation-wide, all employees. Components: integrated quality control · customer focus · employee involvement · continuous improvement · process management · data-driven decisions.

  • KAIZEN = "change for the better" / continuous improvement through small, incremental changes (not revolutionary). Components: Gemba (go and see) · employee involvement · continuous problem solving · standardisation.

  • Six Sigma: listed in the Quick Notes agenda as a quality improvement method alongside KAIZEN (data-driven, DMAIC: define, measure, analyse, improve, control, as covered in Module 1). The deck gives no further detail.
