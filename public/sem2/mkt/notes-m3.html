Module 3 · Pricing Decisions

Principles of Marketing · Notes

NotesCheat sheetMind map

Read this first: The Ebook covers price as value, costs vs customer value, internal and external factors, four market types, demand and elasticity, pricing objectives, cost/buyer/competition approaches, the 6-step pricing procedure, pricing across the PLC, B2B and internet pricing, new-product pricing (skimming, penetration), product-mix pricing and seven price adjustments (discounts, discriminatory, psychological, value, promotional, geographical, international). The Study Notes (LMS quizzes) frame pricing as cost-based, market-based, value-based, dynamic, four objectives (incl. survival), legal/ethical/sustainable pricing, and four methods (cost-plus, skimming, penetration, dynamic).

1. Price, cost and customer value

  • Price = an expression of value: the seller's estimate of what product, image, availability and service are worth to buyers. Glossary: price = exchange value of a good or service.

  • Set price between cost (floor) and customer value (ceiling). Above perceived value → no market; below cost → no survival. The most important determinant = the marketer's objectives.

Costs (Ebook, airline example)

Cost type Airline example Pricing implication
Fixed Annual depreciation of aircraft High fixed + semi-fixed → price for maximum capacity utilisation
Semi-fixed Fuel, crew pay, airport fees (same whether plane empty or full) After fixed costs are covered, each extra sale is mostly profit
Variable Food and beverages (vary with passengers) High variable share (manufacturing) → maximise unit contribution (price − unit variable cost)

  • Pricing below full cost is used to: survive under-utilised capacity (cover variable cost + some overhead), win a large order, or grab market share expecting the experience / learning curve and scale economies to cut unit cost. Risky: losses may never be recovered.

  • Cost is not a "hard" number: full cost vs variable cost vs experience-curve estimates is a managerial judgement.

Customer value (Ebook)

Idea Meaning
Perceived value What the buyer recognises now
Potential value What the buyer can be educated to see (the task of marketing: ads, personal selling, trial)
Segments differ A large firm values supplier tech service less than a small dependent firm
Options Lowest available price sets the upper bound; also the option not to buy (compare via ROI of savings)
Price as a quality signal Pricing far below a functional equivalent can be self-defeating
Price discrimination True value-pricing charges different prices to different groups

  • Cost-plus trade-off: simple and some protection for a low-cost producer, but loses profit = what customers would have paid minus what they paid.

  • Less price sensitive: buyers who can pass on the cost, and buyers uncertain about switching to an untried cheaper supplier.

2. Factors affecting pricing decisions

Internal (controllable, direct) External (uncontrollable, indirect)
Marketing objectives (QB: an internal factor): survival, current profit max, market-share leadership, product-quality leadership. E.g. Paramount Airways business class → high price; no-frills → low Nature of market and demand: market sets the upper limit
Other objectives: price low to deter entry; non-profits: full / partial cost recovery, social price Competition: costs, prices, reactions of rivals
Marketing-mix strategy: price coordinated with product, place, promotion; target costing; non-price positions Other environment: economy (inflation, boom, recession, interest rates), resellers' margins, government (regulated industries), social concerns
Costs: set the floor; economies of scale, experience curve (semiconductors, PCs)
Organisation for pricing: small firms → top management; large firms → product-line managers; industrial → salespeople negotiate within ranges

Watch: In Module 1 "environmental forces are uncontrollable" (Ebook). Here the Ebook again treats external pricing factors as uncontrollable and internal ones as largely controllable.

3. Market types (Ebook)

Market Structure Pricing
Pure competition Many buyers and sellers, uniform commodity Nobody can charge above or below the going price
Monopolistic competition Many buyers and sellers, range of prices Sellers differentiate; buyers pay different prices
Oligopolistic competition Few sellers, highly sensitive to each other's pricing and marketing (QB answer) Product uniform or not; no permanent gain from a cut or hike
Pure monopoly One seller: government (India Post), regulated private (power company), non-regulated private (Sify VPN) Govt: below cost, cost recovery or high price to slow use (demarketing); regulated: fair return; non-regulated: what the market will bear

Demand and elasticity

  • Demand curve = the relation between price charged and resulting demand (QB: True). Normally inverse; for prestige goods a higher price may raise sales.

  • Elasticity = responsiveness of demand to price. Small change in demand → inelastic; large change → elastic.

  • Less price sensitive when (Nagle and Holden): product is distinctive · buyers less aware of substitutes · cannot compare quality · spend is small vs income · small vs total cost of end product · cost shared by another party · used with assets already bought · assumed quality/prestige/exclusiveness · cannot store the product.

  • Most price sensitive to products that cost a lot or are bought frequently.

Competition (Ebook)

  • Commodities: identical prices. Freedom to price above rivals depends on differentiation. Pricing above rivals on purpose = milking (yield share, phase out). Marginal firms that cannot price competitively exit. Large firms may ignore a small rival's cut (short-run cheaper) but risk encroachment. Shortages → opportunistic high prices. Some leaders lead price trends; others follow.

4. Why pricing is centre-stage (Ebook)

  1. Product differentiation blunted by homogenised technology.

  2. Intense inter-firm rivalry (entry/exit barriers removed).

  3. Mature products and markets: differentiate by augmented service or price cuts.

  4. Customers' value perception correlates with price.

  5. Inflation lowers purchasing power and raises input costs.

  • McKinsey (1992, 2,400 firms): a 1% price improvement → 11.1% operating profit gain vs 7.8% (variable cost), 3.3% (volume), 2.3% (fixed cost).

  • Common pricing mistakes: price not revised often enough; set independently of the mix; not varied enough across items, segments, channels, occasions. Russian proverb: "two fools in every market: one asks too little, one asks too much".

5. Pricing objectives

Ebook: four groups

Group What it means
Profitability Profit maximisation (point where added revenue = added cost) and target return (% of sales or investment). Profit = Revenue − Expenses; Revenue = Price × Quantity. 10% price rise with 8% volume fall raises revenue; with 11% fall it reduces revenue
Volume Sales maximisation within a minimum profit, and market-share goals
Meeting competition Match the price leader; compete on non-price variables. Value pricing = benefits vs price and quality of rivals (value ≠ cheap)
Prestige Relatively high price for an image of quality and exclusiveness for status-conscious buyers

Study Notes: four objectives (LMS)

Objective Key idea
Profit maximisation Highest overall profit by balancing revenue and costs; assess fixed and variable costs, break-even; not simply the highest price
Market share growth Competitive prices + promotions (discounts, bundles, loyalty programmes) to expand the customer base
Customer value maximisation Align price with perceived benefits; may mean premium price; long-term relationships, continuous improvement
Survival in the market Adapt prices in downturns for resilience and operational continuity; short-term adjustments plus long-term planning (innovate, diversify, new markets)

Trap: SN KC2: "Survival pricing is short-term without considering long-term sustainability" = False. Misconception: survival pricing is NOT just reactive and short-term.

6. Pricing approaches

Study Notes: four approaches (LMS)

Approach Anchor Steps / features
Cost-based Production costs Analyse variable (change with volume) + fixed costs (rent, salaries, depreciation) → price that covers cost and stays competitive
Market-based External factors 1st: analyse competitors' pricing; 2nd: market demand and supply → pricing sweet spots
Value-based Customer's perceived value 1st: assess perceived value (quality, brand, features, experience); 2nd: align price with customer expectations
Dynamic (surge / demand pricing) Real-time conditions Uses demand fluctuations and seasonal trends; up in high demand, down in low demand; algorithms and data analytics

Ebook: three general approaches

Approach Details
Cost-based Cost-plus / markup (supermarket markups on price: baby food 9%, tobacco 14%, dried foods 27%, greeting cards 50%; higher for seasonal, specialty, slow-moving, high storage, inelastic items). Also break-even / target-profit pricing
Buyer-based Perceived-value pricing: non-price mix builds value; price matches it. Overpricing → poor sales; underpricing → sells well but leaves revenue
Competition-based Going-rate (follow competitors / price leader; common in oligopolies selling commodities) and sealed-bid (price on expected rival bids; not below cost)
Cost-plus: advantages Cost-plus: disadvantages
Covers all costs Based on an assumed activity level; actual volume changes costs
Gives target margin High-cost firm becomes passive on price
Rational, widely accepted Forgoes chances to charge high prices
Simple to understand Ignores price elasticity; unhelpful for penetration or fighting rivals; inflexible
Following competitors: advantages Disadvantages
Simple; follows market trend If rivals are wrong, you are wrong
Relevant to competitive standing Your costs and objectives may differ
Avoids harmful price wars Rival may change price for wrong reasons
  • 3Cs model (Kotler and Keller): costs = floor, competitors' and substitutes' prices = orienting point, customers' assessment of unique features = ceiling.

7. Six-step pricing procedure (Ebook)

  1. Select the pricing objective (profitability, volume, meeting competition, prestige).

  2. Determine demand (demand curve; price sensitivity; statistical analysis, price experiments, surveys).

  3. Estimate costs: experience-curve risk (assumes weak rivals; a rival may innovate cheaper tech) · activity-based costing (ABC) tags real cost of serving each customer · target costing (market price − desired margin = target cost; drop product if it cannot be met).

  4. Analyse competitors' costs, prices and offers (add value of your extra features to the nearest rival's price; subtract theirs).

  5. Select a pricing approach (cost, buyer or competition based: the 3Cs).

  6. Select the final price: other marketing activities, company guidelines, gain-and-risk-sharing, impact on other parties (intermediaries, sales force).

8. Pricing across the PLC (Ebook)

Stage Pricing
Introduction Temporary monopoly, no benchmark; use substitutes as start. Skimming (short term; attracts rivals), penetration (share champion, deters entry, broad demand) or cost-plus. Too high a price can kill the product
Growth Gradually reduce price to skim more price-sensitive segments; experience-curve leader cuts prices to punish late entrants
Maturity Growth levels off; good, better, best versions per segment
Decline Cut price but cut production cost harder; or raise price on last units (replacement parts) and terminate the line
  • Most reliable method: get inside customers' heads; how they value you vs rivals and substitutes matters most.

9. B2B and internet pricing (Ebook)

Industrial pricing features Internet: who can do what
True price ≠ list price (delivery, installation, discounts, training, trade-in, financing) Buyers: instant comparisons (PriceScan, bots), name their price (Priceline), get products free (Open Source, Linux)
Intertwined with product, promotion, distribution; cross elasticities across complements and substitutes Sellers: monitor behaviour and tailor offers (GE Lighting: 300 factors, 30 days → 6 hours), give special prices to loyal buyers (CDNOW secret URL)
Many ways to change price; competitive bidding and negotiation Both: negotiate in online auctions and exchanges (eBay, Rediff air tickets) → era of dynamic pricing
Emphasis on fairness; affected by inflation, interest and exchange rates (risk without an escalation clause)

10. New-product pricing

Market skimming Market penetration
Price High at launch, lowered layer by layer Low at launch to win share quickly
Target Early adopters willing to pay a premium Price-sensitive mass market
Conditions Quality/image support price; enough buyers; small-volume cost not too high; rivals cannot enter easily Highly elastic demand; price-sensitive market; costs fall with experience; low price deters rivals
Benefits Quick R&D recovery; revenue before rivals; segments by price; controls demand; aura of exclusivity Rapid adoption; barriers to rivals; economies of scale; cost leader
Risk Attracts competition (unless patent protected); alienating customers as price falls Short-term profit hit (misconception: no guarantee of long-term profit); customers may leave when price rises

SN KC2 traps: "Skimming attracts price-sensitive consumers with low prices" = False. "Penetration launches at higher prices" = False. "Cost-plus may discourage efficiency and innovation" = True. "Dynamic pricing adjusts on real-time data" = True.

11. Product-mix pricing (Ebook: five situations)

Strategy Meaning
Product-line Price steps between items in a line (cost differences, features, rival prices); price points signal quality levels
Optional-product Optional or accessory items sold with the main product; stripped base models often get rejected
Captive-product Main product cheap, required supplies with high markup. In services = two-part pricing (fixed fee + variable usage)
By-product Sell by-products at any price above storage and delivery cost → main product price can drop
Product-bundle Several products combined at a reduced price

12. Price adjustments (Ebook: seven strategies)

1. Discounts and allowances

Type Given for
Cash discount Paying bills promptly; improves cash, cuts bad debts
Quantity discount Buying large volumes; must not exceed the seller's cost savings (QB: "quantity discount is for paying promptly" = False)
Functional (trade) discount Channel members performing functions (selling, storing, record keeping); same within a channel
Seasonal discount Buying out of season; keeps production steady
Allowances Trade-in (old item turned in) and promotional (dealers join ad and sales programmes)

2. Discriminatory pricing

  • Two or more prices not based on cost differences: customer-segment, product-form, location, time pricing.

Source note: The Ebook presents discriminatory pricing as a normal price adjustment. The Study Notes list price discrimination that exploits vulnerable groups as an unethical practice. For LMS ethics questions, follow the SN.

3. Psychological pricing

  • Price-quality perception (higher price = higher quality when quality cannot be judged) · reference prices (prices buyers carry in mind) · odd pricing (just under round numbers; signals a bargain).

4. Value pricing

  • Right combination of quality and good service at a fair price; often redesigning brands for more quality at the same price or same quality for less.

5. Promotional pricing

  • Lower-than-normal price used temporarily in the selling strategy (QB answer). Forms: loss-leader (cut price on known brands for store traffic), special-event, cash rebates, low-interest financing / longer payment / longer warranties, psychological discounting.

6. Geographical pricing

Method How freight is handled
FOB-origin Title passes at the carrier; customer pays freight from factory (fair but costly for distant buyers)
Uniform delivered Same price plus freight to all, regardless of location; easy; nationally advertised price
Zone Two or more zones; one price per zone; farther zone = higher price
Basing-point Freight charged from a chosen city, whatever the actual shipping point
Freight-absorption Seller absorbs all or part of freight to win a customer or area; used for penetration

7. International pricing

  • Standard worldwide price · dual pricing (domestic vs export; cost-plus or flexible cost-plus) · market-differentiated pricing (set by local conditions; needs fast, accurate information).

  • Case: DHL dynamic pricing. Web tools tested prices on cold callers; most prices went down without matching FedEx/UPS; quote-to-shipment ratio rose from 17% to nearly 25%. Dynamic pricing works best where there is no buyer-seller bond; unique bundles make comparison harder; best framed as a reward (APL: cheaper rates for early, accurate booking).

13. Pricing policies and constraints (Study Notes)

Area Key points
Legal and regulatory Comply with regional, national, international pricing laws. Antitrust laws prevent monopolistic practices: avoid price-fixing and collusion
Ethical pricing Fairness (true value, no deceptive markups → long-term loyalty) and transparency (clear structure, no hidden fees → consumer trust, credibility, reputation); avoid exploiting vulnerable groups
Environmental and social Sustainable pricing reflects environmental impact over the lifecycle: sourcing of raw materials, manufacturing, disposal/recycling. Social: fair wages, community well-being
Market conditions and competition Periodic reassessment (not constant changes); respond to competitive pressure with research and differentiation (features, bundles, targeted promotions)
