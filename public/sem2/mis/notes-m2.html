1. Functional and cross-functional systems

  • IS can be grouped by business function, but in the real world they are integrated combinations of functional systems. Functions: accounting, finance, marketing, production/operations, HRM.

  • e-Business systems describe how IS integrate and support enterprise-wide business processes and the functions of marketing, manufacturing, HRM, accounting and finance (QB answer).

Function Systems (Ebook)
Marketing Interactive marketing, sales force automation, CRM, sales/product management, targeted marketing, advertising, market research
Manufacturing CIM (computer-integrated manufacturing): CAD, manufacturing resource planning, manufacturing execution systems; process control, numerical control, robots
HRM Staffing, training and development, compensation; HR websites and intranets
Accounting Order processing, inventory control, receivables, payables, payroll, general ledger
Finance Cash management, online investment management, capital budgeting, financial forecasting

  • Cross-functional enterprise systems: ERP, CRM, SCM (they also re-engineer processes). Enterprise Collaboration Systems (ECS) support communication and collaboration among teams. Enterprise Application Integration (EAI) software interconnects them.

  • Transaction processing cycle (5 activities): data entry → transaction processing → database maintenance → document and report generation → inquiry processing. Online TPS = real-time processing.

2. Types of information systems by management level

  • Top management: long-term goals, policies, strategic advantage. Middle management: tactical decisions. Lower management: daily transactions. Role evolved from automation of manual processes to rationalisation and integration.

Transaction Processing Systems (TPS)

  • Among the earliest computerised systems. Record, process, validate and store transactions. A transaction = an exchange between two or more business entities (sales, purchases, deposits, refunds).

  • QB: TPS are cross-functional information systems that process data resulting from business transactions.

  • Internal transactions (recruitment, promotion, production policy; department ordering supplies) vs external transactions (sales, purchases; a customer order).

  • Characteristics: records internal and external transactions; routine, repetitive; used by lower-level managers; batch (periodic) or online (as it occurs) mode; six steps: data entry, validation, processing and revalidation, storage, output generation, query support.

  • Features: imposes rules on how to record and store; the data lifeline of the firm (source for MIS and DSS); main link with customers, suppliers, regulators; exists in every functional area. Example: McDonald's supplier orders.

Two lists, both in the Ebook: The transaction processing cycle has 5 activities (entry, processing, database maintenance, documents/reports, inquiry). The steps in processing a transaction are 6 (entry, validation, processing/revalidation, storage, output, query support). Read the question wording.

Decision Support Systems (DSS)

  • Interactive computer-based system that helps decision-makers use data and models to solve unstructured problems; supports managers facing semi-structured and unstructured problems. Couples human intellect with computer capability.

  • Three components: language system (user communication), knowledge system (problem-domain knowledge as data or procedures), problem processing system (link between the two).

  • A DSS: is an IS, used by managers, for decisions, supports, not replaces, people, for semi/unstructured decisions, includes a database and models.

  • SN: DSS functions = data analysis, modelling, forecasting; a marketing manager can simulate scenarios before a product launch.

Source conflict: The Module 2 Ebook says DSS are "generally used by the highest level of management" for unstructured decisions. The Module 1 Study Notes place DSS with managers for semi-structured and unstructured decisions and give top executives to EIS/ESS. The LMS practice follows the Study Notes: DSS = semi-structured problems, scenarios, predictive analytics; ESS/EIS = top executives.

Management Information System (MIS)

  • QB definition: "a planned system of collecting, processing, storing and disseminating data in the form of information needed to carry out the functions of management."

  • A subset of the overall internal controls; used by management accountants to solve problems like costing. MIS "lives" where technology and business intersect. Information is the lifeblood of organisations. Restrictively called information technology management.

  • Philip Kotler: a marketing information system = people, equipment and procedures to gather, sort, analyse, evaluate and distribute timely, accurate information to marketing decision makers.

  • MIS ≠ IS (IS includes systems not meant for decisions) ≠ computer science ≠ ERP (ERP includes non-decision elements). Computers suit quantitative more than qualitative data.

  • Benefits: coordination between departments, quick referencing, access to data, less labour, better techniques, day-to-day management (accounts, stock, payroll), closer contact with the world.

  • SN: MIS is the bridge between operational and strategic levels, giving summarised, structured information to middle management for planning and control.

Executive Support Systems (ESS)

  • QB: ESS supply the necessary tools to senior management. Decisions are never structured, "educated guesses"; executives rely on external data as much as internal. Summarised data with drill-down; graphic presentation.

  • Mintzberg-style manager roles: interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator). ESS are needed mainly for decisional roles: phase 1 identify problems/opportunities, phase 2 decide what to do.

  • ESS do not give ready-made decisions. Flatter organisations push ESS to lower levels.

ESS advantages ESS disadvantages (as listed)
Simple for executives; no deep computer skill Computer skills required to obtain results
Timely company summary information Preparation and analysis time
Better understanding; filters data for time management Hard to quantify benefits; hard to keep database integrity
Improves information tracking Only moderate support of external data and graphics
  • SN: ESS uses dashboards, visualisation and interactive interfaces; real-time data is crucial; deals with unstructured and external data.

3. Functional perspective and enterprise systems

  • Systems are interdependent: TPS are the major producer of information for the others. They are only loosely coupled in most organisations.
Function Responsibility (Ebook)
Sales and marketing Selling the firm's products; identify customers, needs, develop, advertise
Manufacturing and production Actually producing goods and services; facilities, goals, materials, scheduling
Finance and accounting Manage financial assets to maximise return; manage capitalisation; needs external information
Human resources Attracting, developing and maintaining the workforce

QB trap: "The sales and marketing function is responsible for attracting, developing, and maintaining the firm's workforce" is False. That is the HR function.

  • Enterprise systems (ERP) integrate key business processes into a single software system so information flows seamlessly. ES = Packaged Enterprise Application Software (PEAS): ERP, CRM, SCM. Built on platforms like SAP NetWeaver, Oracle Fusion and a relational database. Data warehousing/BI is usually excluded (does not execute processes). ES = software; IS = social system with people.
Benefit area Enterprise system benefit
Firm structure One organisation; disciplined culture
Management Firm-wide knowledge-based management process
Technology Unified platform, single integrated repository
Business More efficient operations; customer-driven processes

  • Challenges (Ebook): daunting implementation, high up-front cost and future benefits, inflexibility (complex, expertise shortage), realising strategic value (generic models can kill unique processes). SN challenges: resistance to change, data migration, training, customisation, cost overruns; fix with change culture, communication and phased implementation.

  • Enterprise Information System: "enterprise class" computing (high quality of service, large data) that removes information fragmentation by a standard data structure.

4. Strategic uses of IS

  • IS help achieve goals only where the organisation has a clear corporate and competitive strategy. An effective strategy fits the organisation; it is not necessarily least cost.

  • QB: "The successful application of strategy in the contemporary global environment requires an organization to have an effective strategic management process."

  • Firm level: core competency = what a business does better than anyone (best jeans, fastest flowers); IT improves it by sharing information across units. Industry level: competitive forces and network economics (AOL vs Microsoft: compete and cooperate) and information partnerships (tyre makers share standard sizes; co-branded credit cards).

Porter's competitive forces and the Internet

Force Internet effect (Ebook)
Threat of new entrants Increases: no sales force or storefront needed, remote rivals
Supplier power Mixed: buyers find substitutes, but linked suppliers lock in customers
Buyer power Increases: more information, lower switching costs
Substitutes Threat to information-dependent industries
Rivalry Increases: harder to keep systems secret, price competition

  • Harmon et al. (2001) suggest a sixth force: bargaining power of employees. Porter: the Internet does not change the model; it is a complement to traditional competing.

  • Mechanisms of competitive advantage: barriers to entry, switching costs, lower production costs, product differentiation, control of distribution channels, innovation, quality control. Pioneers take a risk if new technology fails.

  • Sources of entry barriers: economies of scale and scope, differentiation, capital requirements, cost disadvantages, distribution access, government policy.

  • QB lines: "Using technology to become the least-cost producer gives an advantage over the competition." "Monitoring production lines and analyzing data are important aspects of quality control" (True).

SN: competitive advantage and IT governance

  • IS differentiate a business (analytics on customer preferences; Tesla). IS must align with business goals (global expansion needs scalable systems).

  • IT governance = frameworks and processes for decisions on IT investments, policies and implementation, so IS initiatives align with business goals and resources are not misallocated. Example: a bank adopting blockchain for security and transparency.

5. Economic, organisational and behavioural impacts

  • Transaction cost theory: IT reduces the cost of buying in the market what the firm cannot make, so firms can shrink in size.

  • Agency theory: the firm is a "nexus of contracts" among self-interested individuals; principal (owner) hires agents (employees) who need supervision. IT shifts the agency cost curve down and to the right, so firms grow while lowering agency cost.

  • Behavioural theories: IT lowers information costs and broadens distribution, so it may remove middle managers or push decisions to lower-level workers. Flatter hierarchies; exodus of middle managers.

  • SN economic impacts: cost savings and revenue generation. ROI compares gains against costs (CRM ROI = retention, sales, lower marketing cost).

  • SN organisational/behavioural impacts: changing work patterns and roles (flexibility, remote collaboration, upskilling); culture and structure (fewer hierarchical barriers; resistance to change).

6. IT and decision-making

  • Decisions happen at all levels: board (strategic), managers (tactical), employees (own tasks).

  • Danger: decisions based on wrong information; managers assume a situation is familiar (new packaging "as good" because same colour).

  • Good IS: flexible, support varied styles, powerful models, reflect bureaucratic and political needs, appreciate the limits of change.

  • Systems analysis = what the system should do; system design = how it will do it.

  • Technology improves decisions through information (central databases), collection (POS links), process (data must reflect decision processes), tools (BI software across preparation, structuring, making, management stages) and groups (video conferencing).

  • SN decision support tools: data visualisation, predictive analytics (forecast from historical data), scenario analysis (simulate conditions). Software: Tableau (visualisation), IBM Watson Analytics (AI, predictive), Microsoft Power BI (share insights). EHR in healthcare; POS data in retail.

7. Value chain, core competencies and SIS

  • Value chain analysis is the most common business-level analytic tool; it highlights where competitive strategies and IS have the most strategic impact.
Primary activities Support activities
Inbound logistics · operations · outbound logistics · sales and marketing · service Firm infrastructure · human resources · technology (development) · procurement

  • SN: technology adds value at each stage: automated inventory (inbound), process automation and quality control (operations), tracking (outbound), CRM (marketing), support systems (service), e-procurement, R&D tools, HRIS.

  • SCM (SN): end-to-end from raw material to customer; RFID and IoT give real-time tracking; analytics for demand forecasting; cloud SCM for partner collaboration; cuts lead times and cost.

  • Core competencies (SN): unique capabilities, skills, knowledge and technologies that distinguish a firm. Examples: Apple (innovation and design), Amazon (supply chain and logistics), Toyota (lean production).

  • MIS (Ebook keyword): the discipline focused on integrating computer systems with the aims and objectives of an organisation. Institute of MIS; MIS Quarterly.

Strategic Information Systems (SIS)

  • QB: an SIS "helps companies change or otherwise alter their business strategy and/or structure"; speeds reaction to environmental change for competitive advantage.

  • Key features: DSS aligning IS/IT with strategy; ERP; databases with data mining; real-time systems.

  • IS are strategic if they support the competitive strategy, change the way a firm competes, or change industry structure.

  • Competitive advantage = ROI above the industry average. Indicators: market share, sales, new customers, loyalty, lower production/service costs, reputation.

  • Successful SIS: innovative and unique, not easily copied, built on pre-existing resources (skills, patents, brand), top management support, strategic partnerships, analysable by frameworks. Strategic behaviour is hard to observe (litigation fear, imitation, failures not discussed).

  • SN: SIS are aligned with strategic objectives and long-term success, not routine operations. Examples: Netflix recommendation algorithm, Wal-Mart supplier-linked inventory (low cost), Delta Airlines CRM. Measure by alignment, competitive advantage, innovation, customer satisfaction, financial performance.

Exam traps: e-Business systems = enterprise-wide integration · ESS = tools for senior management · SIS = alter strategy/structure · TPS = cross-functional, transaction data · MIS = collect, process, store, disseminate · least-cost producer → advantage over competition · quality control = monitoring production lines · HR (not marketing) = attracting and maintaining workforce · IT governance = align IT with business goals · ROI = compare costs and benefits.

Clinical analogy: A hospital ERP is the HIS that links pharmacy, billing, lab and wards; the cath-lab inventory module is SCM; your referral CRM is how a cardiology practice keeps referring physicians engaged.
