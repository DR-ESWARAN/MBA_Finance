Module 5 · Measures of Central Tendency

Quantitative Techniques · Notes

NotesCheat sheetMind map
  • Clark & Sekkade: "An average is an attempt to find one single figure to describe the whole of figures"
  • Murray R. Spiegal: "Average is a value which is typical or representative of a set of data"

Functions of an Average (3):

  1. Present huge data in summarised form
  2. Facilitate comparison between datasets
  3. Help in decision making

2. Characteristics of a Good Average (7)

  1. Rigidly defined — preferably by algebraic formula
  2. Easy to compute
  3. Easy to understand
  4. Based on all observations
  5. Capable of further algebraic treatment
  6. Not unduly affected by extreme observations
  7. Not much affected by fluctuations of sampling

3. Types of Averages

Category Types
Mathematical Averages Arithmetic Mean, Geometric Mean, Harmonic Mean, Quadratic Mean
Positional Averages Median, Mode
Commercial Averages Moving Average, Progressive Average, Composite Average

Exam focus: Mathematical + Positional only

4. Arithmetic Mean (AM)

Definition

Sum of observations ÷ Number of observations

Calculation Methods

Situation Method
Individual observations Direct method OR Short-cut method (d = X − A, where A = assumed mean)
Ungrouped freq. distribution Direct: X̄ = ΣfX/Σf; Short-cut: X̄ = A + Σfd/Σf
Grouped freq. distribution Use mid-values as representative; same formula as ungrouped

Weighted Arithmetic Mean

  • Used when items have unequal importance (weights)
  • Formula: X̄w = ΣwX / Σw
  • Applications: index numbers, standardised rates

Properties of AM (7)

  1. Sum of deviations from mean = 0 (mean is centre of gravity)
  2. Sum of squared deviations is minimum from mean (least squares property)
  3. Algebraically treatable
  4. Combined mean: X̄ = (N₁X̄₁ + N₂X̄₂) / (N₁ + N₂)
  5. Adding/subtracting constant B from all values → mean shifts by B
  6. Multiplying/dividing by constant b → mean multiplied/divided by b
  7. Replacing observations changes mean by average change in magnitude

Merits of AM (8)

  1. Rigidly defined by formula
  2. Easy to compute (addition + division only)
  3. Based on all observations
  4. Capable of mathematical treatment — most widely used
  5. Can be computed from sum + count alone
  6. Least affected by sampling fluctuations
  7. Centre of gravity — balances positive and negative deviations
  8. Good basis for comparing two distributions

Demerits of AM (7)

  1. Cannot be located by inspection or graphically
  2. Cannot be computed for qualitative data
  3. Too affected by extreme values — major limitation
  4. May give fictitious value not in the dataset
  5. Cannot be computed with open-end class intervals
  6. Different distributions may have same mean (misleading)
  7. Gives greater weight to larger values

5. Median

Definition

Value that divides the distribution into two equal halves; positional average (depends on position, not magnitude)

Determination

  • Individual obs: Arrange in ascending/descending order → middle value
    • Odd n: Median = (n+1)/2 th value
    • Even n: Median = mean of (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th values
  • Grouped freq distribution: Use interpolation formula
  • Graphically: Intersection of less-than and more-than ogives → drop perpendicular → X-axis point = Median

Properties (2)

  1. Positional average
  2. Sum of absolute deviations is minimum when taken from median

Merits (7)

  1. Easy to understand and compute; locatable by inspection
  2. Can be determined even with open-end classes
  3. Not affected by extreme observations
  4. Can be located graphically (ogive)
  5. Centrally located — sum of absolute deviations is minimum
  6. Only suitable average when data are qualitative and rankable
  7. Conveys the idea of a typical observation

Demerits (7)

  1. Arrangement of data required — cumbersome for large data
  2. Not capable of algebraic treatment (positional)
  3. For even n — median is estimated, not an actual observation
  4. Not based on all observations — different sets may give same median
  5. More affected by sampling fluctuations than mean
  6. Assumes uniform distribution in median class (rarely true)
  7. Weighted median not defined — unsuitable for unequal importance items

Uses (5)

  1. When characteristics are not measurable but rankable
  2. To convey idea of a typical observation
  3. Best for skewed distributions (e.g., income — positively skewed)
  4. When quick estimates of average are needed
  5. When class intervals have open ends

6. Mode

Definition

Value that occurs the maximum number of times in a distribution; most densely concentrated value

  • Croxton & Cowden: "Value at the point around which items tend to be most heavily concentrated"
  • A.M. Tuttle: "Value with greatest frequency density in its immediate neighbourhood"

Determination

  • By Inspection: Direct — value with max frequency (only when distribution is regular)
  • By Grouping: When distribution is irregular; prepare 6-column analysis table
  • Modal class for grouped data: Use interpolation formula

Bi-modal: Two values with maximum frequency Multi-modal: More than two modes

Merits (6)

  1. Easy to understand; locatable by inspection
  2. Can be located when variable is not measurable but rankable
  3. Not affected by extreme observations
  4. Can be determined with open-end classes
  5. Can be determined with unequal class widths (if modal class and neighbours are equal width)
  6. Best representative of data — maximum concentration

Demerits (7)

  1. Not based on all observations
  2. Not capable of mathematical treatment
  3. Not rigidly defined in all cases
  4. Much affected by sampling fluctuations
  5. Difficult to calculate with small datasets
  6. Not suitable when items have unequal importance
  7. Unstable — depends on choice of class interval width

7. Relation Between Mean, Median and Mode

Distribution Relationship
Symmetrical Mean = Median = Mode
Positively skewed Mode < Median < Mean
Negatively skewed Mean < Median < Mode

Empirical relationship (for moderately skewed): Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean

8. When to Use Which Measure?

Situation Best Average
Normal, symmetric data Mean
Skewed data (e.g., income) Median
Most typical value needed Mode
Open-end class intervals Median or Mode
Qualitative + rankable data Median
Further algebraic treatment needed Mean

High-Yield Exam Points

  • 7 Characteristics of good average — MCQ staple
  • Properties of AM — sum of deviations = 0; combined mean formula
  • AM demerits: Extreme values; fictitious average; open-end classes
  • Median: Best for skewed distributions; only average for qualitative rankable data
  • Mode: Bi-modal, Multi-modal concepts; Croxton & Cowden definition
  • Empirical relationship: Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean
  • Symmetric distribution: Mean = Median = Mode
