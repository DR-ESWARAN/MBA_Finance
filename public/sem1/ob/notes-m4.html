Module 4 · Power, Politics, Conflict & Negotiation

Organizational Behavior · Notes

NotesMind map

Max Weber: "The probability that one actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his own will despite resistance."

Emerson: "The power of actor A over actor B is the amount of resistance on the part of B which can be potentially overcome by A."

Working Definition: An interpersonal relationship in which one individual (or group) has the ability to cause another to take an action that would not be taken otherwise.

  • Involves implied force to comply — not necessarily actual force
  • Person A has power over B if B believes A can force compliance

2. Power vs Authority vs Leadership

Concept Definition Basis
Power Ability to secure compliance Individual or group capacity — may be informal
Authority Right to seek compliance; backed by legitimacy Formal position + group acceptance
Leadership Ability to elicit responses that go BEYOND required compliance Voluntary — inspires, not just directs
  • Power = "informal authority"; Authority = "legitimate power"
  • Headship vs Leadership: Department head has right to require; leader has ability to inspire

3. Types of Power (Etzioni's 3)

Type Basis Org Example
Coercive Force to comply Prison organization
Utilitarian Performance-reward contingency Business organizations
Normative Members' belief in org's right to govern behaviour Religious organizations

4. Bases of Power — French & Raven's 5

Power Base Source Employee Response
Referent Power Personal qualities, charisma, admiration — B follows A because B admires A Commitment
Expert Power Knowledge/expertise relevant to B; narrow domain credibility Commitment
Legitimate Power B submits because A has a RIGHT to exert power in a domain; another name for authority Compliance
Reward Power A controls rewards B wants (pay, promotions, desirable assignments) Compliance
Coercive Power Based on FEAR; A can punish B; also called punishment power Resistance

Clinical analogy: Referent power = how a legendary cardiologist influences a trainee through admiration alone, not rank. Expert power = how a specialist commands authority in their narrow domain even over senior administrators.

Legitimate power derives from:

  1. Prevailing cultural values (e.g., age-based power in Japan/Korea)
  2. Accepted social structure (royal families)
  3. Designation (board appoints CEO; promotion to manager)

Coercive power examples: Fire, demote, transfer to undesirable jobs, strip perquisites. "The power to hurt others is possibly the most often used, most often condemned and most difficult to control." — Kipnis

5. Behavioural Consequences of Power

Power Type Used Employee Response
Referent / Expert Commitment — motivated to follow; believes in leader's cause; exerts considerable energy
Legitimate / Reward Compliance — follows because it's duty or reward expected; no enthusiasm
Coercive Resistance — sees no reason to cooperate; engages in tactics to defeat leader

Factors affecting Power Dependency:

  1. Subordinate's values — if outcomes A controls are important to B, B is more susceptible to influence
  2. Nature of A-B relationship — peers vs superior-subordinate; permanent vs temporary job
  3. Counterpower — B's other sources of power that buffer A's influence (e.g., union membership)

6. Political Behaviour in Organizations

Lasswell's definition: Politics = "who gets what, when, and how"

Pfeffer's definition: "Those activities taken within organizations to acquire, develop, and use power and other resources to obtain one's preferred outcomes in a situation in which there is uncertainty."

Power vs Politics (Pfeffer):

  • Power = force, store of potential influence — property of the system at REST
  • Politics = power in ACTION; activities to develop + use power in organizational settings

Political behaviour need NOT be dysfunctional — can serve organizational interests. Politics is not inherently bad.

When is Political Behaviour GREATEST vs LEAST?

Greatest Politics Least Politics
High uncertainty & complexity Low uncertainty & complexity
Scarce resources, high competition Ample resources, little competition
Non-programmed decisions Programmed decisions with clear rules
Ambiguous goals Specific, well-defined goals

7. Reasons for Political Behaviour (5)

  1. Ambiguous goals — room to pursue personal gain under guise of org goals
  2. Limited resources — need to use politics to claim one's "share"
  3. Changing technology & environment — dynamic environment → ambiguity → political behaviour
  4. Non-programmed decisions — ambiguous conditions leave room for political manoeuvring
  5. Organizational change — restructuring, new divisions → different factions fight over territory

8. Political Strategies in Intergroup Relations

Resource Dependence Model:

  • Subunit that controls a scarce resource needed by another subunit gains power
  • "The rich get richer" — power from resources is used to obtain more resources → more power

Strategic Contingencies (Miles):

  • "A requirement of the activities of one subunit that is affected by the activities of other subunits"
  • Controlling strategic contingencies = power even without controlling resources directly
  • Example: Business office of a university has power over schools via approval/veto of financial expenditures
  • Classic study (Lawrence & Lorsch): Successful firms' power distribution aligned with strategic contingencies
    • Container manufacturing → power in Sales & Production (customer delivery + product quality)
    • Food processing → power in Sales & Research (marketing + food science expertise)

9. Conflict Management — Ways to Resolve Conflict

Method How
Change the Structure Align goals of conflicting units; report to same executive; evaluate both on shared outcomes
Change Team Composition Separate conflicting personalities; change physical layout (antagonists side-by-side = less conflict than across from each other)
Common Opposing Force Unite conflicting groups against a common enemy (competitor, recession)
Majority Rule Vote on the issue; works if process is perceived as fair; don't overuse
Problem Solving Focus on the PROBLEM not each other; find root cause; both sides partially right

Research: Antagonists seated ACROSS from each other → more conflict. Seated SIDE BY SIDE → less conflict.

10. Conflict-Handling Styles (5 Styles)

Style Assertiveness Cooperation Behaviour Best when
Avoidance Low Low Deny conflict exists; postpone decisions Issue is trivial
Accommodation Low High Give in; sacrifice personal goals for relationship Others' interest is more important
Compromise Medium Medium Each side sacrifices something; middle ground When time-pressed; moderate stakes
Competition High Low Win regardless of others' feelings; push for own outcome Strong moral objection; ethical issue
Collaboration High High Both sides argue + listen; seek WIN-WIN; challenge ideas not people Complex issues; long-term relationship
  • No single "right" style — depends on situation
  • Collaborative style has potential to be highly effective in many situations
  • Managers tend to prefer forcing (competition); subordinates tend to avoid, accommodate, or compromise
  • Healthy conflict: Needed when no conflict may mean self-censorship and withheld opinions

Clinical analogy: Avoidance = ignoring a cardiac finding hoping it resolves; Collaboration = multidisciplinary team discussion for a complex case — best outcome.

11. Negotiation

Definition: Process by which individuals/groups attempt to realize goals by bargaining with another party who has at least some control over goal attainment.

4 Stages of Negotiation

Stage Description Cultural note
1. Non-task time Get to know each other; small talk; no focus on the task North Americans keep it short; Mexico/S. Korea use it to build the relationship
2. Information exchange Exchange background and general info; set context Japan: very important stage — proposals need context
3. Influence & Persuasion Use promises, threats, questions to reduce other's demands and increase acceptance of yours Effectiveness depends on relative power + cultural/industry norms
4. Closing Agreement or failure to agree USA: signed contract = deal is done; China: signed contract = beginning of final negotiations

2 Bargaining Strategies

Strategy Type Characteristics
Distributive Bargaining Win-Lose Fixed resources; maximise own share; usually short-term relationship (e.g., buyer-seller of house)
Integrative Bargaining Win-Win Both parties try to find creative solutions that benefit both; based on mutual problem-solving (e.g., bilateral trade negotiations)

12. Organizational Environment & Structure

Open vs Closed Systems

System Characteristic
Open System Responsive and adaptive to environmental changes; feedback loop: Input → Throughput → Output → Environment
Closed System Less sensitive to environmental resources and possibilities
  • Model: Open system takes in resources (Input) → processes (Throughput) → releases outputs → back to environment
  • Example: US Big 3 auto makers in 1980s acted as closed systems → failed to adapt to Japanese competition

4 Building Blocks of Organizational Structure

Building Block Meaning
Centralization Degree to which decision-making authority is concentrated at higher levels
Formalization Extent to which policies, procedures, job descriptions, and rules are written and explicitly articulated
Departmentalization How jobs are grouped — Functional (by similarity) or Divisional (by product/geography/customer)
Span of control (implied) — reporting relationships defining hierarchy

6 Types of Organizational Structures

  1. Functional — grouped by function (marketing, finance, HR, IT)
  2. Divisional — grouped by product, service, geography, or customer
  3. Matrix — combination of functional and divisional; dual reporting
  4. Geographic — separate departments for different regions/countries
  5. Networked Team — cross-functional teams with less formal hierarchy
  6. Virtual — geographically dispersed; connected by technology
