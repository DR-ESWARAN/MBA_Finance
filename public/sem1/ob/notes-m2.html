Module 2 · Diversity, Decision Making & Motivation

Organizational Behavior · Notes

NotesMind map

Definition: Similarities and differences among employees in terms of age, cultural background, physical abilities/disabilities, race, religion, gender, and sexual orientation.

  • Makes the workforce heterogeneous
  • Employing a diversified workforce = necessity today; managing it = challenge
  • Diversity = strength IF managed properly → increases productivity

Key Drivers of Diversity (3 forces):

Driver What it means
Women in Workplace Highest-ever female participation; dual-income families, single working mothers
Organizational Restructuring Downsizing + outsourcing → HRM disruption; fewer people doing more
Legislation Equal opportunity laws make workplace discrimination illegal; both employer and employee rights defined

Benefits of Diversity:

  • Mutual respect among employees → synergistic work environment
  • Economic empowerment of marginalized workers
  • Reduces lawsuits, increases marketing opportunities, recruitment, creativity, business image
  • Competitive edge + higher productivity
  • Better financial performance in diverse firms (research confirmed)

Negative attitudes as barriers:

  • Prejudice, stereotyping, discrimination → harm morale, productivity, and invite litigation
  • Must NEVER be used in hiring, retention, or termination

Clinical analogy: A cardiac team with diverse specialists (interventionalist, echo expert, heart failure cardiologist) outperforms a homogeneous group. Diversity of expertise = better outcomes.

2. Challenges of Managing Diversity

  • More than acknowledging differences — involves recognizing VALUE of differences
  • Combating discrimination + promoting inclusiveness
  • Managers need multicultural skills: self-awareness, understanding biases, willingness to change

Required Tools for Managers:

  1. Understand discrimination and its consequences
  2. Recognize own cultural biases/prejudices
  3. Remember: diversity is about individual differences, not group stereotypes
  4. Be willing to change the organization
  5. Create "safe" space for communication
  6. Provide mentoring programs for access to information and opportunities

3. Recommendations for Managing Diversity

A. Interview Selection Process

  • Use highly structured interviews to avoid race/gender bias
  • 15 characteristics of structured interviews (key ones):
    • Same questions for all candidates
    • Job analysis basis
    • Multiple rating scales (anchored)
    • Multiple interviewers
    • Detailed notes
    • No discussion between interviews
    • Statistical prediction
  • Similarity bias — interviewers prefer candidates similar to themselves → mitigated by structured interviews

B. Diversified Mentoring Relationships

  • Mentor ≠ mentee in terms of race, gender, class, disability, sexual orientation, or status
  • Mutually beneficial — both gain knowledge, empathy, cross-group interaction skills

C. Diversity Training Programs

  • Evolved from compliance-only (Civil Rights Act 1964) → now focuses on:
    1. Building cultural competencies
    2. Valuing differences
    3. Learning how diversity improves business decisions
  • Perspective-taking + goal-setting activities → pro-diversity attitudes persist months later
  • Sexual orientation now included in corporate diversity training

4. Industrial Relations (Brief)

  • IR = whole gamut of relationships between employers and employees
  • Managed through conflict and cooperation
  • Includes: employment conditions (pay, hours, leave, safety), disciplinary actions, retirements, collective bargaining, workers' participation in management
  • Harmonious IR = cordial, regular communication, grievance resolution at all levels

5. Managerial Decision Making

Definition: Choosing among alternative courses of action to achieve organizational goals.

5-Step Decision Making Process (EDIAE):

Step Name Key point
1 Establish the Objective Private firms → profit max; public firms → benefit-cost criterion
2 Define the Problem Identify root cause (e.g., why profits declining?)
3 Identify Alternatives List all possible solutions
4 Evaluate Alternatives Use regression, linear programming, cost-benefit analysis; choose optimum under constraints
5 Implement the Decision Monitor continuously; take corrective action if needed

Constraints on decisions: Legal, financial, infrastructure/raw material availability, technological

6. Barriers to Effective Decision Making

Barrier Meaning
Bounded Rationality Cannot process ALL information — brain has limits; especially true for non-programmed decisions
Escalation of Commitment Tendency to keep investing in a bad decision even when outcomes are negative
Time Constraints Pressure → reliance on heuristics → suboptimal decisions
Uncertainty Cannot know outcome before choosing; must commit without full information
Personal Biases "Similar to me" bias; Confirmation bias (favoring info that confirms existing beliefs)
Conflict Discomfort with conflict leads to avoiding necessary but uncomfortable decisions

Confirmation bias = paying more attention to information that confirms existing beliefs, ignoring contradictory evidence.

Clinical analogy: Escalation of commitment = a surgeon persisting with a failing procedure instead of converting to an alternative — sunk cost fallacy in the OR.

7. Improving Quality of Decision Making

A "good" decision = RIGHT person + BEST available evidence + SOUND process (NOT judged by outcome alone)

7 Strategies:

  1. Learn from experience — record decisions + outcomes; reflect regularly
  2. Entertain doubt — test decision against multiple "what if wrong?" scenarios
  3. Give yourself options — optimal: 3–4 alternatives (fewer = dilemma; more = paralysis)
  4. Argue it out — assign team members to argue FOR each option; appoint a "red team" to challenge preferred view
  5. Understand context — go to base information, not bullet summaries; use multiple modes (talk, read, visit, observe)
  6. Try experiments — pilots/prototypes → win-win (success = confidence; failure = prevent full-scale disaster)
  7. Pre-mortem (Gary Klein) — pretend decision already failed; ask "what caused this?" → improve or find better decision

Listening > Talking in group decisions — leader who speaks first biases the whole discussion; hold your opinion until others have spoken.

8. Work Motivation for Performance

6 Ways to Improve Performance & Motivation (Vroom's Expectancy Theory foundation):

# Strategy Key Principle
1 Make Expectations Clear Employees need goals + measurable standards (Expectancy Theory: know what to do & how judged)
2 Provide Continuous Feedback Goal-setting theory: progress feedback = motivating; be specific, not generic
3 Correct Privately; Praise Publicly Public correction = demotivating; public praise = positive reinforcement
4 Believe in Your Employees "I know you can do better" > "you're a screw-up"; trust is a key of transformational leadership
5 Make Rewards Achievable Vroom's expectancy: reward must be seen as POSSIBLE; avoid one-person-wins-all structures
6 Let People Know What They're Fighting For Purpose drives motivation when "how" is unclear; people join a crusade, not a company

9. Process Theories of Motivation

Definition: Explain WHY behaviours are initiated — focus on mechanism of target selection and effort.

4 Process Theories:

Theory Core Idea Key Term
Operant Conditioning Do what is rewarded; avoid what is punished "Law of Effect"
Equity Theory Motivation driven by perceived fairness vs. others Input/Output comparison
Goal Theory People attempt to achieve goals they INTEND to achieve; goals → greater motivation and effort Intention drives effort
Expectancy Theory Exert effort to achieve valued outcomes; asks (a) which alternative to choose? (b) how motivated to pursue? Effort → Performance → Reward

Strongest theory: Goal Theory — "one of the strongest in OB" (per Ebook) Most comprehensive: Expectancy Theory

Clinical analogy (Equity): If a junior registrar is paid the same as a senior consultant for the same work → senior consultant's motivation drops instantly. Perceived inequity destroys engagement.

10. Performance Appraisal

Claims FOR Performance Appraisal:

  • Gives direction — aligns individual goals with organizational goals
  • Gives feedback — formal forum for performance discussion
  • Identifies training needs — surfaces areas of weakness
  • Links reward to performance — motivates, attracts & retains high achievers
  • Sets motivating goals — challenging but achievable targets stretch performance

Arguments AGAINST Performance Appraisal (Deming):

  • Performance = Effort + Ability + System Capability + Training/Experience → individual appraisal ignores system factors
  • Halo Effect — reputation biases the appraiser
  • Personal bias — appraiser's likes/dislikes color assessment (recognized since 18th century China)
  • Lack of reproducibility — same person rated differently by two different managers
  • Targets are unworkable:
    • Too low → cap on aspiration; no incentive to exceed
    • Too high → cheating the measurement system or blame-shifting
    • "Aiming for mediocrity" when targets are negotiated
  • Performance measurement should improve the system, not just evaluate individuals

Deming's key point: If targets are used to evaluate individuals (not improve systems) → negative reactions that undermine performance.
