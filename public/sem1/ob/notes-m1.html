Module 1 · Introduction to OB

Organizational Behavior · Notes

NotesMind map

Definition (Robbins): "Systematic study of actions and attitudes that people exhibit within organizations."

Definition (Keith Davis): "Study and application of knowledge about how people act within organizations — a human tool for human benefit."

3 Core Elements:

  • Impact of individuals on behaviour
  • Impact of groups on behaviour
  • Impact of organizational structure on behaviour → Goal: Apply this knowledge to improve organizational effectiveness

Scope of OB:

  • Culture, change management, conflict & stress
  • Organizational development, group behaviour, power & politics
  • Job design, transactional analysis, study of emotions

Contributing Disciplines:

Discipline Contribution to OB
Psychology Learning, motivation, personality, leadership, job satisfaction, attitude
Sociology Group dynamics, roles, communication, norms, power, conflict
Social Psychology Change management, group decision-making, social norms
Anthropology Cultural differences, cross-cultural behaviour, mergers/acquisitions
Political Science Govt regulations, organizational politics, stability

2. Behaviour Model for Organizational Efficiency

A manager must be able to do 4 things with human behaviour (DUPC):

Function Meaning
Describe Identify and articulate how people behave
Understand Know why people behave a certain way
Predict Anticipate future behaviour based on patterns
Control Channel behaviour toward organizational goals

Clinical analogy: Like a cardiologist — you describe the ECG pattern, understand the pathophysiology, predict risk, and then intervene (control). OB is the same for human behaviour in organizations.

3 Elements of any Organization: People → Structure → Technology

3. Individual & Cultural Differences in Perception

Perceiver Characteristics — two people see the same person differently because of:

  • Cognitive accessibility — what traits/attributes they find salient (e.g., fashion vs. athletic ability)
  • Need for cognition — some people think deeply about others; others judge quickly
  • Attributional style — entity theorists vs incremental theorists (see below)

Entity Theorists vs Incremental Theorists:

Entity Theorist Incremental Theorist
Belief Personality is fixed Personality can change
Attribution tendency Personal (internal) attributions Situational (external) attributions
Under pressure Make personal attributions easily Better at using situational context
Challenge response Struggle (can't adapt) Optimistic (believe they can adapt)

4. Cultural Differences in Person Perception

Culture Type Examples Focus Attribution tendency
Individualistic (Western) USA, Canada, Australia The individual Internal/personal attributions
Collectivistic (East Asian) Japan, China, India, Korea Relationship/situation Situational attributions

Key Study (Morris et al.): Bicultural Hong Kong students primed with American icons made more personal attributions; primed with Chinese icons → more situational attributions.

5. Attributional Styles & Mental Health

Attributional Style = the type of attributions we habitually make for events that happen to us.

3 Dimensions of Attribution:

  • Internal vs External — due to me OR due to situation
  • Stable vs Unstable — permanent OR temporary
  • Global vs Specific — applies to everything OR only this situation

Negative Attributional Style (pessimistic):

  • "I failed because I am no good" → Internal
  • "I always fail" → Stable
  • "I fail at everything" → Global
  • → Leads to hopelessness, depression

Positive Attributional Style:

  • "Task was difficult" → External
  • "I'll do better next time" → Unstable
  • "I failed here but I'm good elsewhere" → Specific
  • → Leads to persistence, resilience

Learned Helplessness:

  • Extreme negative attribution → person believes they have NO control over outcomes
  • Originated in Seligman's dog experiments (shocks → passive behaviour even when escape possible)
  • Results in poor health outcomes, low performance

Self-Handicapping:

  • Creating external excuses before failing (e.g., taking performance-inhibiting drug in study)
  • Short-term ego protection → Long-term: lower satisfaction, competence, mood, substance abuse risk
  • More common in men than women

6. Perception & Job Attitudes

What drives Job Satisfaction & Organizational Commitment?

Factor Key Point
Personality Positive affective disposition → higher satisfaction; neurotic → lower
Person-Environment Fit Skills match job demands + values match company values = higher satisfaction
Job Characteristics Skill variety, autonomy, feedback, task significance = satisfaction
Psychological Contract Unwritten expectations — breach → low satisfaction
Organizational Justice Fair policies, fair treatment = satisfied employees
Relationships at Work Good manager & peer relations = strong predictor of satisfaction
Stress Role ambiguity/conflict → dissatisfaction; time pressure/responsibility can be positive
Work-Life Balance Flexibility policies → higher satisfaction

Psychological Contract = Unwritten understanding of what employee brings vs what company gives. Breach = dissatisfaction.

Consequences of Positive Work Attitudes:

  • Higher engagement, motivation
  • Moderate positive correlation with performance (r ≈ 0.30)
  • Stronger link in professional jobs (engineering, research) vs manual jobs
  • Lower absenteeism & turnover

How Companies Track Attitudes:

  • Attitude surveys — periodic, must be confidential
  • Exit interviews — understand why people leave

7. Learning

Definition: Permanent change in behaviour due to direct/indirect experience.

Factors Affecting Learning (4M):

  • Motivation — drive to achieve goal
  • Practice — repetition builds skill
  • Environment — internal (family/culture) + external (school/workplace)
  • Mental group — peer group shapes thinking

4 Learning Theories:

Theory Key Idea Example
Classical Conditioning (Pavlov) CS paired with US → CR Bell + food → salivation to bell alone
Operant Conditioning (Skinner) Behaviour controlled by its outcomes Reward/punish to shape behaviour
Social Learning (Bandura, Rotter) Learn by observing others + reinforcement Watching a mentor and imitating
Cognitive Learning Deliberate thinking + mental structures Problem-solving based on past knowledge

Clinical analogy: Social learning = how a medical intern learns procedures. Watches (observes) → assists (imitates) → performs independently (reinforced).

8. Reinforcement

Positive Reinforcement: Add desirable stimulus → behaviour increases

  • Primary reinforcers: food, water (biological needs)
  • Secondary reinforcers: money, praise, status (conditioned)
  • 4 Principles: Contingent → Immediate → Size → Deprivation

Negative Reinforcement: Remove unpleasant stimulus → behaviour increases

  • ≠ Punishment (punishment decreases behaviour; negative reinforcement increases it)
  • Example: Manager stops criticizing → employee maintains improved performance

Schedules of Reinforcement:

Schedule Basis Example Effect
Fixed Interval Time — fixed Monthly salary Predictable
Variable Interval Time — varies Random spot checks Sustained behaviour
Fixed Ratio # of responses Piece-rate pay High rate, then pause
Variable Ratio # of responses — varies Slot machine Highest & most persistent response

Variable Ratio = most powerful schedule — why gambling is addictive.
