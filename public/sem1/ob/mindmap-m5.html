2. Coping with Work-Related Stress
Good Stress vs Bad Stress
Good: Keeps you focused, energetic, alert — meets challenges
Bad: Exceeds coping ability → damages mind, body, job satisfaction
5 Common Causes
Fear of being laid off
Overtime due to staff cutbacks
Pressure to perform, no job satisfaction increase
Pressure to perform at optimum levels ALL the time
Lack of control over how you do your work
Warning Signs (know ≥4)
Anxious, irritable, or depressed
Apathy, loss of interest in work
Problems sleeping; fatigue
Trouble concentrating; muscle tension/headaches
Social withdrawal; loss of sex drive
Using alcohol or drugs to cope
5 Tips to Manage Stress
Tip 1: Reach out — share stress; co-worker & family support network
Tip 2: Exercise & Nutrition — 30 min aerobic; small frequent healthy meals; Omega-3s; no nicotine; moderate alcohol
Tip 3: Sleep — 8 hours; consistent schedule; screens off 1 hr before bed
Tip 4: Prioritize & Organize — balanced schedule; leave earlier; regular breaks; healthy boundaries; don't over-commit
Tip 5: Break bad habits — resist perfectionism; flip negative thinking; don't control the uncontrollable; use humour; clean workspace
Clinical analogy: Stress follows the Starling curve — optimal stress improves performance (well-loaded ventricle); excessive stress causes decompensation (heart failure).
3. Overview of Entrepreneurship
3 Key Thinkers & Definitions
Jean Baptiste Say (19th c.): Create value by shifting resources from lower- to higher-valued activities
Schumpeter (20th c.): Innovators driving "creative destruction" — reforming patterns of production
Drucker (21st c.): Search for change, respond to it, exploit it as an opportunity
7 Elements of Entrepreneurship
1. Recognizing change
2. Pursuing opportunity
3. Taking on risk and responsibility
4. Innovating
5. Making better (higher value) use of resources
6. Creating new value meaningful to customers
7. Doing it all over again and again
Why Entrepreneurs Do It
Only consistent predictor: One or both parents were entrepreneurs
Pursue passion / deep interest
Create something new; enhance reputation; make an impact
Desire for independence; be your own boss
Flexible lifestyle; financial rewards
4. Intrapreneurship & Risk
Intrapreneurship
Definition: Applying entrepreneurial mindset (innovation, risk-taking, flexibility) within an existing organization
Objective: React to market opportunities quickly like start-ups
Example: GE encourages intrapreneurship to accelerate new product development
Social entrepreneurship = entrepreneurship focused on value to society
Risk, Resilience & Persistence
Most start-ups fail before 4 years (US Small Business Administration)
Internal failure: Limited funding, poor planning, bad idea
External failure: Weak economy, changing public policies
Resilience example: Steve Jobs removed from Apple → returned to transform it (iPod, iPhone, iPad)
First reason for a business plan: Test whether idea is a truly economically promising market opportunity
5. Optimal Government Support for Entrepreneurship
A. Reducing Barriers (Less Discouragement)
Regulations: Clear, stable rules; reduce unnecessary restrictions; reduce red tape
IP protection: Patent system + rule of law → entrepreneurs feel more secure
Taxes: Lower taxes → more willingness to risk capital; progressive tax penalizes success
Cultural barriers: Outlaw discrimination; education campaigns; subsidize childcare
Trade barriers: Tariffs protect incumbents; disadvantage new entrants
Skype vs FWD: FWD waited 18 months for US regulatory clearance → Skype built user base globally → FWD went out of business
B. Increasing Positive Incentives
Technology transfer: Give govt-produced technology to private sector for commercialization
Financing: Govt loans, subsidized lending/borrowing
Venture capital: Where private sector won't risk (risk: props up low-quality businesses)
Training: Formal entrepreneurship courses; business incubators; networking events
Long-term pipeline: Financial literacy education; entrepreneurship culture from elementary school
Risk of govt support: Cronyism — favoritism to industries not best-positioned economically
