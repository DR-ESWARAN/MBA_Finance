Module 4 · Pricing Decisions

Managerial Economics · Notes

NotesMind map

3 Major Influences

  1. Customers — willingness to pay; price hike may drive them to substitutes
  2. Competitors — rival reactions; aggressive competitor forces price down; no competitor → higher price possible
  3. Costs — lower cost relative to price = more supply; product priced below cost drains resources

External Factors (11)

  1. Total demand and price elasticity
  2. Number of competing products/services
  3. Quality of competing products
  4. Current prices of competing products
  5. Customer's preferences (quality vs price)
  6. Sole source vs heavy competition
  7. Seasonal vs continual demand
  8. Life of product or service
  9. Economic and political climate
  10. Type of industry and future outlook
  11. Government guidelines

Internal Factors (9)

  • Cost base (variable / full / total / standard)
  • Target return on investment
  • Loss leader vs main product
  • Quality of inputs (materials/labour)
  • Labour-intensive vs automated process
  • Markup percentage
  • Usage of scarce resources
  • Firm's profit and other objectives
  • Short-term vs long-term pricing horizon

2. Pricing Methods

Method Basis Key Use Limitation
Full Cost Plus (Total Cost Plus) All costs (direct + indirect) + profit margin Long-term contracts, regulated industries Ignores demand; circular reasoning if volume changes
Manufacturing Cost Plus Manufacturing cost only + higher profit margin When selling to cover non-mfg overhead too Doesn't include admin/selling costs
Conversion Cost Plus Labour + overhead (no material) + markup When customer provides material Rarely used; narrow applicability
Marginal Cost Plus Variable costs only + markup Special orders, spare capacity, new markets, price wars Fixed costs not guaranteed to be covered
Differential Cost Plus Increase in total cost for extra units + markup One-time extra orders Variable AND some fixed included
Standard Costs Pre-determined efficient cost + markup Faster pricing; avoids inefficiency absorption Outdated standards = wrong prices

Full Cost Plus — 3 valid situations:

  1. Long-term government/custom contracts (price = full cost + markup mandated)
  2. Long-term customer relationships (all activity costs emerge over time)
  3. Fluctuating demand — SR prices based on incremental costs; LR average = full cost price

3. Short-Run vs Long-Run Pricing

Dimension Short-Run Long-Run
Time horizon ≤ 6 months Multi-year
Cost basis Incremental costs only Full (all activity) costs
Use case One-time special orders with no repeat impact Standard pricing policy
Risk Capacity must be available; can't set precedent Must cover all costs or firm makes losses

Conditions for SR special order pricing (Colin Drury):

  1. Sufficient spare capacity available
  2. Bid price will NOT affect future pricing expectations
  3. Capacity released after short period for more profitable use

4. Target Pricing — 5 Steps

  1. Develop a product satisfying customer needs
  2. Choose target price based on customers' perceived value + competitors' prices
  3. Derive target cost = Target Price − Desired Profit Margin
  4. Estimate actual cost of the product
  5. If actual > target cost → investigate cost reduction; redesign; or accept smaller margin

Formula: Target Cost = Target Price − Target Profit

5. Life Cycle Costing & Pareto Analysis

Life Cycle Costing: Tracks all costs from initial R&D → end of customer service/support. Prices must cover entire lifecycle costs.

Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule — Vilfredo Pareto):

  • 80% of sales revenue comes from 20% of products
  • 20% cost drivers cause 80% of total costs (ABC costing)
  • Application areas: Stock control, customer profitability, quality control, pricing in multi-product firms, ABC costing
  • Use: Design pricing strategies for high-revenue 20% products; convert low-performing 80% into profitable lines

6. Economic Approach to Pricing

  • Profit is maximised where MR = MC
  • Firm produces as long as MR > MC
  • Ideal price: MR of last unit = MC of last unit
  • Two practical problems: (1) Difficult to estimate demand curve (hundreds of products); (2) Demand depends on quality, packaging, ads — not just price

7. Price Indifference Point

  • Sales level at which net income is same under two pricing alternatives
  • If expected sales at new price > indifference point → accept price change (profit increases)
  • If expected sales at new price < indifference point → reject price change (profit decreases)
  • Useful for short-term pricing decisions

8. Price Discrimination

Definition: Charging different prices to different customers/groups for the same product based on their willingness to pay.

3 Types by Nature

Type Definition Example
Personal Different price per individual Doctor charging rich vs poor patients differently
Geographical Different price in different markets Dumping — sell cheaply abroad, high price domestically
By Use Different price for different uses of same product Electricity: domestic (low) vs commercial (high)

3 Degrees of Price Discrimination

Degree Name How Example
1st Degree Perfect / Personalised Charge maximum each individual is willing to pay; captures all consumer surplus Client-specific professional fees
2nd Degree Product Versioning / Menu pricing Different price for different quantities consumed Bulk discounts
3rd Degree Group Pricing Different price per consumer group Movie tickets: adults/seniors/children — MOST COMMON

5 Conditions for Price Discrimination

  1. Different elasticity in different segments (high price where inelastic)
  2. Market imperfections — seller must be able to divide the market
  3. Differentiated product — same service but different uses
  4. Legal sanction — e.g., electricity board pricing
  5. Monopoly power — requires control over market

9. Market Structures — Comparison

Feature Perfect Competition Monopoly Monopolistic Competition Oligopoly
Sellers Many One Many Few (2–7 dominate)
Product Homogeneous No close substitutes Differentiated Differentiated or homogeneous
Price control Price TAKER Price MAKER Price MAKER (limited) Price MAKER (interdependent)
Barriers to entry None High Low High
Equilibrium P = MR = MC MR = MC; P > MR MR = MC; SR profit → 0 LR Sticky prices; collusion

Perfect Competition

  • Price determined by market demand–supply intersection (Marshall: "both blades of scissors")
  • Individual firm's demand curve = horizontal line at market price (perfectly elastic)
  • Equilibrium point E: Quantity demanded = Quantity supplied at price OP

Monopoly

  • Single producer; no close substitutes; downward sloping AR (= demand) curve
  • MR < AR (MR falls twice the rate of AR)
  • Equilibrium: MR = MC (maximises profit)
  • Formula: MR = AR[(e−1)/e] where e = price elasticity
  • Numerical (from ebook):
    • P = 1000 − 10Q; TC = 100 + 40Q + Q²
    • MR = 1000 − 20Q; MC = 40 + 2Q
    • MR = MC → Q = 44, P = 120, Profit = ₹20,844
  • When MC = 0 → equilibrium at midpoint of AR where elasticity = 1

Monopolistic Competition

  • Many firms, differentiated products, low barriers
  • Price makers but demand highly elastic (price-sensitive consumers)
  • SR: positive economic profit → firms enter → LR: profit → 0
  • Heavy advertising to differentiate; may result in wasteful rent-seeking

Oligopoly

  • Few large firms; mutual interdependence
  • Sticky prices — change less frequently than other structures
  • Non-collusive: independent pricing; price war possible; price settles between monopoly (upper) and competitive (lower) limits
  • Collusive: firms act as single unit; joint profit maximisation cartel or market-sharing cartel
  • Price leadership: one firm (low-cost / dominant / barometric) sets price; others follow
