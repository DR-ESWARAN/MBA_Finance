> **Read this first:** *Ebook chain: **product design → value → market
> size → volume → capacity → process**. Capacity planning should rest
> solely on **maximising value delivered to the customer** (low cost, on
> time, highest quality). It is a strategic tool guiding long-term
> choices on capacity, location and layout.*
>
> **Wording note:** *The LMS Study Notes for this module keep saying
> "**Management Information Systems**" where they mean
> operations/capacity management. Read it as POM; if an LMS question
> uses "MIS", it still means this topic.*

**1. Defining capacity**

| **Ebook term**           | **Meaning**                                                                                               |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Theoretical capacity** | Ideal conditions, **short period**; no breakdowns, maintenance, set-ups, bottlenecks, worker errors       |
| **Normal capacity**      | Max output when plant runs an **average period** on a **normal mix**. A plant can run at **\>100%** of it |
| **Effective capacity**   | What a firm can expect given product mix, scheduling, maintenance, quality standards (utilisation)        |
| **Efficiency**           | **Actual output ÷ effective capacity** (as %)                                                             |
| **Rated capacity**       | **Maximum usable** capacity = capacity × utilisation × efficiency                                         |

| **LMS Study Notes term** | **Meaning**                                              |
|--------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Design capacity**      | Theoretical maximum under ideal conditions (upper limit) |
| **Effective capacity**   | Allows for real constraints: breakdowns, maintenance     |
| **Actual capacity**      | Real-time output under prevailing conditions             |
| **Utilisation (SN)**     | **Actual output ÷ design capacity × 100**                |

> **Source difference:** *Ebook uses theoretical / normal / effective /
> rated. LMS uses **design / effective / actual**. Ebook efficiency =
> actual ÷ **effective**; SN utilisation = actual ÷ **design**. Match
> the term in the question.*

- **Capacity = max production rate per hour × hours worked per period.**
  Production rate = units ÷ time. Capacity changes with hours, rate or
  product mix.

> **Worked example (Ebook):** *Efficiency 90%, utilisation 80%, **3
> lines**, 6 days × 3 shifts × 8 h = **144 h/week**, 100 products/h per
> line. Rated capacity = 100 × 3 × 144 × 0.8 × 0.9 = **31,104
> products/week**.*

- Problems in measuring: **product mix** (top management states new
  capacity in **money value of sales**) and **time** (thermal power
  plant must run 24×7 or not at all; most factories measure output per
  shift). In the end capacity comes down to **individual machine
  capacity** → work centre → plant.

**2. Measuring capacity**

- First task: **define the unit of output**. RIL Hazira: 250,000 MT
  polypropylene, 160,000 MT polyethylene; power plant: MWh.

| **Setting**             | **Capacity measure**                     |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Airline                 | Seat miles                               |
| Hospital                | **Bed-days** per year (SN: beds per day) |
| Restaurant              | Customers per day / seats                |
| Process-focused         | Size: beds, seats                        |
| Repetitive              | Units per shift (refrigerators)          |
| Product-focused (TISCO) | Tonnes of steel per shift                |

| **Output measures**                                                           | **Input measures**                                                   |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Usual choice for **high-volume** processes                                    | Used for **low-volume, flexible** processes                          |
| Maruti 100,000 cars/yr (avg: 125,000 if only 800, 110,000 Omni, 85,000 Gypsy) | Machine shop: machine hours or number of machines                    |
| Less useful as customisation and variety rise                                 | Demand (an output rate) must be converted to input; can be difficult |

**3. Determinants of effective capacity**

- Capacity plans: set time and resources to meet demand · strategies for
  new requirements · cost of non-conformance (waste, slippage, quality
  variance).

- Ebook 7 determinants: **facilities · product and service factors ·
  process factors · human factors · operational factors · supply chain
  factors · external factors**.

- SN highlights: **facility design and layout** (layout efficiency,
  fewer bottlenecks) and **technology and equipment** (automation,
  predictive maintenance).

> **Clinic:** *Cath lab design capacity might be 12 cases/day. Effective
> is 9 after cleaning and set-up. Actual is 7 if a C-arm fails.
> Utilisation (SN) = 7 ÷ 12 = 58%; efficiency (Ebook) = 7 ÷ 9 = 78%.*

**4. Planning capacity requirements**

- Address the external environment first (Indian Airlines raises
  frequency → Jet must respond or lose share, but risks
  under-utilisation). Then demand for product lines, new technology, new
  products.

1.  Forecast demand for each product in each line

2.  Calculate the array of assets needed

3.  Project availability of existing assets over the horizon

- **Informed capacity decisions need knowledge of present resources and
  bottlenecks** (system capacity). High confidence in forecast needed.
  If shortage is 2 years away and building takes 18 months, start
  planning in 6 months. Invest until marginal efficiency of capital =
  interest rate.

- **Capacity gap analysis** tells **when demand will exceed capacity and
  by how much**; timing and sizing are linked.

- **Capacity cushion**: 80% average utilisation → **20% cushion**. Large
  cushions where demand is highly variable, resources inflexible,
  service important.

**Tactics to match capacity to demand**

4.  Add people (overtime, extra shift)

5.  Motivate employees (incentives, involvement)

6.  Adjust equipment and processes (buy, sell, lease)

7.  Redesign product for throughput

8.  Improve equipment operating rate (scheduling, better raw material)

- **System capacity**: to increase it, raise capacity of **only the
  bottleneck**. Outsource (US banks → Indian book-keeping) or share
  facilities (Indian Airlines and Alliance Air swap aircraft).

- Capacity \> demand: cut prices, market harder, change product. Demand
  \> capacity: raise price, long lead times, drop marginal business;
  long term = add capacity. **Best operating level** = utilisation that
  **minimises average unit cost**. RIL: worsted spinning plant in 8
  months, PFY in 14 months.

> **Case: Dalmia Cements** *Doubling from **5.5 to 12 million tonnes**
> by FY2009 via 3 greenfield plants: Kadapa (AP), Ariyalur (TN), Cuttack
> (Orissa); \>\$500 m. 7th largest cement firm; "cement is a local
> business"; turnover Rs 1,200 cr → Rs 2,000 cr by 2009-10.*

**5. What kind and how much**

- **What kind**: technological/engineering question vs
  economy-of-scale/business question. Optimal capacity minimises unit
  cost; low cost = entry barrier.

> **How-much example (Ebook):** *750 units/week, 5 machines, 40 h week.
> Machine 1: actual time = 40 × 0.82 = 32.8 h; normal time = 0.05 ×
> 110/100 = 0.055 h; need 0.055 × 750 = **41.25 h** → 41.25 ÷ 33.6 =
> **1.227 machines** → 2 machines (excess) or 1 + ~9 h overtime. **Ebook
> inconsistency:** it computes 32.8 h available but divides by 33.6.*

- Seasonality: Jet Airways and puja/winter peaks. Compare cost of
  **overage** (too much) vs **underage** (too little).

| **Expansionist strategy**                                                                       | **Wait-and-see strategy**                                            |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Large, infrequent** jumps; stays **ahead** of demand                                          | **Smaller, frequent** increments; **lags** demand                    |
| **Minimises** sales lost to insufficient capacity                                               | Uses overtime, shifts, outsourcing, stock-outs, deferred maintenance |
| Economies of scale, faster learning, pre-empts rivals; needs credibility                        | Suits fast-changing technology; can **erode market share**           |
| RIL: world-scale capacity on latent demand; 1994 polyester conversion 18 c/lb, 60% below rivals |                                                                      |

**6. Decision trees**

- **Most commonly used** tool in capacity planning; lay out options,
  outcomes, risks and rewards. **Square = decision; circle = uncertain
  outcome.** Probabilities at each circle sum to 100% (from data or
  **best guess**). Work **right to left**; subtract cost; keep the best
  value at each branch.

> **Mehta Store (5 years; strong growth 55%, weak 45%):** *Move:
> 180×5−150 = 750; 110×5−150 = 400 → **592.5** · Expand: 150×5−80 = 670;
> 90×5−80 = 370 → **535** · Wait: 60+150×4−90 = 570; lie low 60×5 = 300
> → **448.5**. Best = **Move to Lajpat Nagar** (also best downside).
> **Ebook inconsistency:** text says move costs Rs 200 lakh but the
> working uses 150; with 200 the EV is 542.5, still best.*

- Processing requirement example: A 4,800 + B 4,800 + C 4,500 = **14,100
  hours**.

**7. Make or buy**

| **Item type**   | **Meaning**                                                                   | **Example**                                                    |
|-----------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Proprietary** | Supplier's design, used without change                                        | Headlights, dashboard instruments                              |
| **Standard**    | Universally designed for general use                                          | Fasteners                                                      |
| **Specialty**   | Specialised supply                                                            | Tyres                                                          |
| **Commodity**   | Standard specs or customised by supplier; large investment, separate industry | Steel for car maker; coal, iron ore, limestone for steel maker |

- Rest = **core vs non-core** (relative). TELCO: own foundry and forge
  at Jamshedpur; Pune forge not warranted; now mostly outsourced. Auto
  making went from most integrated to most disaggregated. Nokia Beijing:
  zero inventory, all parts from suppliers.

- Basis: **true internal cost vs cost of buying**. Three dimensions:
  **strategic** (TELCO forge ensured steering quality) · **operational**
  (nearest forgers Wyman Gordon, Bharat Forge on west coast) ·
  **organisational** (integration attractive at **high input volumes**).

- **Virtual factory**: production at suppliers/partners in many places;
  firm only integrates the steps.

- SN: make-or-buy weighs **cost, internal expertise, quality control,
  strategic impact**. Misconception: outsourcing is **not** solely about
  cost.

**8. Developing capacity alternatives (Ebook 6)**

9.  Design **flexibility** into systems

10. Consider **life-cycle stage**

11. Take a **big-picture (systems)** approach

12. Prepare for capacity **"chunks"**

13. **Smooth out** capacity requirements

14. Identify the **optimal operating level** (lowest unit cost)

- **Economies of scale** also from location clusters (cheap transport,
  skilled labour, ancillaries) and shared technology/standards;
  **diseconomies** beyond a point (hotel example). SN adds **FMS**
  (Flexible Manufacturing Systems): adapts quickly but does not remove
  all challenges.

**9. Service capacity**

- Services cannot be inventoried and are produced and consumed together,
  so firms must **build for maximum demand** and use **demand
  management** to shift users off-peak. High customer interaction →
  uncertain processing time; need space for customers.

- SN Part 2 challenges: **intangibility** (hard to quantify), **customer
  involvement** (too much can hurt efficiency),
  **perishability/variability**. Optimising strategies: **demand
  forecasting · flexibility · efficient scheduling · cross-training ·
  inventory as buffer** (not just stockpiling).

> **Case: Apollo Hospitals OPD** *60% of arrivals are OPD patients, 40%
> visitors; 2.2 people per car, 1 patient; 50% need registration (5
> min); 6 sq ft seat; 90% seats usable; 60-min stay; 30 doctors = **180
> patients/h**. System capacity = **72 patients/h**; **bottleneck =
> registration**. Fix: more registration attendants; push demand to lean
> hours; engage waiting patients.*

**10. CVP analysis**

- Shows how profit changes with volume, cost and price. Uses: which
  products to push · volume for target profit · revenue to avoid loss ·
  whether to raise fixed cost · discretionary budgets · fixed-cost risk.

- **Profit = TR − (TVC + TFC)** = (P − V) × Q − F. Contribution margin
  per unit = P − V. **Q = (F + profit) ÷ (P − V)**. **CMR = (P − V) ÷
  P**; **Revenue = (F + profit) ÷ CMR**. Break-even: set profit = 0.

> **Shirt example (ABC Apparels):** *P = 800, V = 300, F = 55 lakh,
> target profit 2 lakh. Q = 57 lakh ÷ 500 = **11,400 shirts**; CMR =
> **0.625**; revenue = **Rs 91.2 lakh**. Break-even = **11,000 shirts /
> Rs 88 lakh**.*
>
> **Trading:** *Break-even shirts are like the number of winning trades
> you need just to cover brokerage and data fees (fixed cost). Every
> trade after that is profit, at the contribution margin per trade.*

**11. Misconceptions (LMS)**

15. Design capacity is **not** always achievable

16. Outsourcing is **not** decided on cost alone

17. **100% utilisation is not ideal** (maintenance cost, no flexibility)

18. Expansion does **not** guarantee immediate returns

19. FMS does **not** remove all challenges

20. Intangibility **does** affect service capacity planning; more
    customer involvement is not always more efficient; flexibility is
    needed; inventory is a buffer, not just stockpiling

> capacity × utilisation × efficiency · output measures = high volume;

> bottleneck = registration · CMR = (P−V)/P.*
