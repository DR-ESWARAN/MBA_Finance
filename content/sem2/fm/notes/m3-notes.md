**1. Ratio analysis: meaning and purpose**

- Ebook: process of ascertaining financial ratios that indicate the
  **ongoing financial performance** of a company; values come from the
  **same company's financial statements**.

- Compares line items to reveal **profitability, liquidity, operational
  efficiency and solvency**; tracks performance **over time** and
  against others in the **same industry**. Lays the **framework for
  financial analysis**.

- Helps the analyst (3): deeper analysis of profitability, liquidity,
  solvency, efficiency · projections and estimates · **cross-sectional
  analysis** against best industry standards.

**Study Notes additions**

- **Ratio** = quantitative metric of the relation between **two
  accounting figures**; expressed as fraction, proportion or %. Used
  mainly by **external analysts**. Key part of **fundamental equity
  analysis**.

- Two main financial statements: **Balance Sheet (positional
  statement)** and **Profit & Loss Account (income statement)**. Study =
  **analysis** (systematic arrangement) + **interpretation** (meaning of
  the facts).

- Ways to use ratios (3): **over time** (trend) · **across companies**
  (same industry) · **against benchmarks** (internal targets; lenders'
  loan **covenants**).

> **Analogy (memory aid):** *Ratios are the lab panel of a company. A
> single value means little; the trend, the comparison with peers and
> the reference range tell you whether the patient is improving.*

**2. Liquidity ratios (short-term solvency ratios)**

- Liquidity = how quickly assets convert to money to pay **short-term**
  obligations. Used by **prospective creditors and lenders**. Examples:
  **cash, current, quick** ratios.

- Expressed in **pure ratio form** (Objective QB). Two basic measures of
  liquidity = **current ratio and quick ratio** (Objective QB).

**Current ratio (working capital ratio)**

- **Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities**. Current
  assets convert to cash within **a year**; current liabilities payable
  within a year.

- **Ideal = 2 : 1** (current assets double current liabilities).

- Ebook example: Coca-Cola 2014 = 32,986 ÷ 32,374 = **1.02**; PepsiCo
  2014 = **1.14**. PepsiCo higher every year, so better placed;
  Coca-Cola above 1, "not bad".

- Weakness (Ebook): assumes **liquidation** of all current assets; a
  going concern should look at the **time to convert** working capital
  to cash (the **cash conversion cycle**). The current ratio can be
  "**misleading**".

- SN limits: poor for comparing **different industries**; imprecise
  (includes hard-to-liquidate assets). A rising trend may signal a
  turnaround.

| **Current assets (SN)**                              | **Current liabilities (SN)**                      |
|------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Sundry debtors · bills receivable                    | Sundry creditors · bills payable                  |
| Cash and bank · marketable securities                | **Bank overdraft** · short-term loan              |
| Stock / inventories · loose tools                    | Income received in advance · outstanding expenses |
| Short-term loans and advances · **prepaid expenses** | Provision for taxation · unclaimed dividend       |

- SN example: CA = 60,000 + 30,000 + 10,000 = 1,00,000; CL = 30,000 +
  20,000 + 10,000 = 60,000 → **5 : 3**.

**Quick ratio (acid-test / liquid ratio)**

- Most liquid current assets ÷ current liabilities. **More
  conservative** than current ratio because it **excludes inventory**
  (and other hard-to-cash items).

- Ebook formula: (Cash & equivalents + short-term investments + accounts
  receivable) ÷ current liabilities, i.e. **Quick assets ÷ Current
  liabilities**.

- Ebook example: Quick assets = Current assets − (**inventories +
  advance tax**) = 1,34,000 − 54,000 = **80,000**; ÷ 1,04,000.

- SN: Quick assets = Current assets − (**stock + prepaid expenses**).
  Ideal quick ratio = **1**. Liquid liabilities = CL − (bank overdraft +
  advance received).

- If current ratio is **much higher** than quick ratio, current assets
  depend on **inventory**. Quick ratio still assumes receivables
  liquidate, so it shares the current ratio's weakness (less so).

**Cash ratio**

- **Cash ratio = (Cash + cash equivalents + invested funds) ÷ Current
  liabilities**. An **extreme** liquidity ratio; refines both current
  and quick ratios.

- Ebook example 1: (34,390 + 12,000) ÷ 73,780 = **0.63**. Example 2:
  treasury bills are marketable securities, so (21,720 + 18,500) ÷
  82,960 = **0.48** (receivables excluded).

- SN drawbacks: rarely used in reporting; too much cash = poor asset
  use; ignores seasonality; \< 1 may signal trouble or an expansion
  strategy.

| **Ratio** | **Numerator**                                        | **Strictness**            |
|-----------|------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Current   | All current assets                                   | Least strict; ideal 2 : 1 |
| Quick     | Current assets − inventory (− prepaid / advance tax) | Stricter; ideal 1 : 1     |
| Cash      | Cash + equivalents + marketable securities           | Strictest                 |

> **Knowledge Check (SN):** *A **solvency** ratio measures ability to
> pay current debt = **False** (that is liquidity) · fixed assets
> convert to cash quickly = **False** · quick ratio = CA / CL =
> **False** · cash ratio uses cash and marketable securities = True.*

**3. Debt ratio (leverage)**

- Leverage ratios show the relative **debt load**. **Debt ratio
  (debt-to-assets) = Total debt ÷ Total assets**.

- Ebook example: total assets = 5,00,000 + 8,45,000 = 13,45,000; total
  debt = 3,40,000 + (2,70,000 − 20,000) = 5,90,000 → **0.44**.
  **Accounts payable are excluded** because they are not debt.

- SN: \> 1 (100%) = more liabilities than assets (default risk if rates
  rise); \< 1 = **equity** finances more of the assets. Interpretation
  depends on **industry** (utilities, pipelines run higher debt than
  technology).

- Financial leverage = **use of more debt capital to increase profit**
  (Objective QB). Leverage also called **gearing** (SN).

**4. Interest coverage ratio (Study Notes)**

- **ICR = EBIT ÷ Interest expense**. Also called **times interest
  earned**. Used by lenders to judge risk; EBITDA version also used.

- ICR of **1.5 or less** = may not meet interest. SN example: EBIT
  8,58,000 ÷ interest 3,00,000 = **2.86 times**.

**5. Profitability ratios**

- Ebook: ability to **create earnings**; compare revenue with groups of
  expenses. Examples: **contribution margin, gross profit, net profit**
  ratios. Overall profitability ratios are based on **investments**
  (Objective QB).

- SN list: gross profit · operating profit · net profit · return on
  assets · return on equity · **EPS** · return on investment · inventory
  turnover. Higher = better; best used **comparatively**.

**Gross profit ratio**

- **GP ratio = Gross profit ÷ Net revenue from operations × 100**.
  Earnings from sales **before administrative expenses**.

- Ebook: Gross profit = Sales + Closing stock − Opening stock −
  Purchases − Direct expenses. SN: GP = Net sales − COGS; COGS = Opening
  stock + Purchases + Direct expenses − Closing stock; Net sales = Sales
  − Sales return.

- Significance (Ebook): gross margin on products · margin to cover
  operating and non-operating expenses · change from selling price or
  cost or both · **low ratio = unfavourable purchase and sales policy**
  · higher is always a good sign. A **decline** may signal **pricing
  pressure**.

- Ebook example: revenue 1,00,000; net purchases 73,000; cost of revenue
  = 73,000 + decrease in inventory 10,000 + direct expenses (carriage
  2,000 + wages 5,000) = 90,000; GP 10,000 → **10%**. **Salaries are not
  direct expenses**.

- Objective QB: sales 1,20,000, GP 30,000 → **25%**. GP should be
  adequate to cover **selling and administrative expenses (all)**.

**Inventory turnover ratio**

- Turnover ratios = **efficiency / performance / activity** ratios:
  ability to use assets to generate sales. Turnover = times assets are
  **turned into sales**.

- **Inventory turnover = Cost of revenue from operations (COGS) ÷
  Average inventory**; **Average inventory = (Opening + Closing) ÷ 2**
  (can be monthly or quarterly).

- High = goods sell faster. Low = weak sales, **excess inventory**, risk
  of **obsolete** stock.

- Ebook example: COGS = 18,000 + 46,000 + 14,000 + 4,000 − 22,000 =
  60,000; avg inventory = 20,000 → **3 times**. SN: 5,40,000 ÷ 1,80,000
  = **3**.

> **Watch out:** *The Ebook places inventory turnover under **turnover
> (activity) ratios**; the Study Notes list it under **profitability**
> ratios. Accept either if the option set forces it.*

**6. Advantages and limitations (Ebook)**

| **Advantages (6)**                                                                  | **Limitations (5)**                                                                    |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Shows efficacy of **operating, investing, financing** decisions                     | False impression of **precision and finality** of accounting data (biggest limitation) |
| Summarises info; assesses managerial efficiency, creditworthiness, earning capacity | Assumes **stable money**; ignores **price-level changes**                              |
| Trends help **projections**                                                         | Only **monetary** aspects; ignores qualitative factors                                 |
| Identifies weak areas and strengths                                                 | Different **accounting policies** spoil cross-firm comparison                          |
| Supports **SWOT**                                                                   | Forecasting from history alone is not feasible                                         |
| Comparison with benchmarks over periods and firms                                   |                                                                                        |

- Weakness in financial statements carries into the ratios.

- Objective QB: ratio analysis is useful in financial analysis, helps
  **communication and coordination**, and helps **planning and
  forecasting** (A, B, D). It **does** help identify weak spots.

**7. Tricky Objective QB logic**

| **Situation**                                      | **Answer and why**                                                                                                                                                       |
|----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Current ratio = 1                                  | Net working capital = **nil** (CA = CL)                                                                                                                                  |
| Which improves current ratio?                      | **Payment to trade payables** (when CR \> 1, cutting CA and CL by the same amount raises the ratio). Collecting receivables or buying goods for cash leaves CA unchanged |
| CR stays 3 : 1 but quick ratio falls 2 : 1 → 1 : 1 | **Higher stock** (inventory now makes up more of current assets)                                                                                                         |

**8. Common misconceptions (LMS)**

| **Wrong belief**                                           | **Correct**                                          |
|------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Financial statements are only for management and investors | Also creditors, government, suppliers, customers     |
| Statements are only annual                                 | Also quarterly or half-yearly                        |
| Ratio analysis is only for external analysts               | Management uses it for planning and benchmarks       |
| Cash ratio is the only quick-settlement test               | Current and quick ratios too                         |
| A high debt ratio always means instability                 | Depends on **industry**                              |
| Ratios alone are enough                                    | Combine with other metrics and qualitative factors   |
| Profitability ratios stand alone                           | Combine with efficiency ratios and industry averages |
