## PART A: DIAGRAMMATIC PRESENTATION

### 1. Why Diagrams?
- Understand trends at a glance; facilitate comparison of various situations

---

### 2. Advantages of Diagrams (6)

1. **Attractive & impressive** — lasting impression; used by newspapers, advertising
2. **Simplify complex data** — intelligible form
3. **Give more information** — reveal hidden facts not visible in tables
4. **Save time and labour** — data grasped quickly
5. **Useful in comparisons** — comparing over time or across places
6. **Universal applicability** — economics, business, administration, social institutions

---

### 3. Limitations of Diagrams (9)

1. Give only vague idea — not suitable for expert analysis
2. Supplement to tabulation, NOT alternative
3. Limited information vs classification and tabulation
4. Very low level of precision
5. Helpful only for comparisons; no further analysis
6. Can portray limited number of characteristics
7. **Liable to be misused** — can present illusory picture
8. Meaningless when measurements have wide variations
9. False base line diagrams must be analysed carefully

---

### 4. General Rules for Making Diagrams (7)

1. Appropriate title and footnote
2. Attractive presentation — neatly drawn, proper proportion
3. Accuracy — use proper scales; accuracy > attractiveness
4. Selection of appropriate diagram type
5. Index — when different shades/colours used
6. Source-note — for secondary data
7. Simplicity — understandable even by a layman

---

### 5. Choice of Diagram — 3 Factors
1. Nature of data
2. Purpose of the diagram
3. Calibre (education level) of the audience

---

### 6. Types of Diagrams

#### Bar Diagrams (One-dimensional — 14 types)
| Type | Use |
|------|-----|
| **Line Diagram** | Large number of items; not attractive |
| **Simple Bar** | Individual series — time or spatial |
| **Multiple (Compound) Bar** | Comparing 2+ sets of data side-by-side |
| **Sub-divided (Component) Bar** | Parts of a whole — absolute values |
| **% Sub-divided Bar** | Parts as percentage of total (each bar = 100) |
| **Deviation Bar** | Net quantities — profit/loss, surplus/deficit (above/below X-axis) |
| **Duo-directional Bar** | Aggregate of 2 components — one above, one below X-axis |
| **Sliding Bar** | % version of duo-directional — each bar = 100% |
| **Pyramid Diagram** | Population distribution by sex, age, education |
| **Broken Scale Bar** | One/few values extremely high vs majority low |
| **Histogram** | Frequency distribution — class intervals on X-axis, freq on Y-axis |
| **Frequency Polygon** | Join mid-points of histogram rectangles; compare 2+ distributions |
| **Frequency Curve** | Smooth curve joining vertices of frequency polygon |
| **Ogive (Cumulative Freq Curve)** | Cumulative freq distribution; used to find median, quartiles |

> **Histogram note:** Height of each rectangle = frequency density (frequency/width) when class intervals are unequal.

> **Frequency Polygon vs Histogram:** Polygon better for comparing 2+ distributions simultaneously.

> **Ogive uses:** Median, quartiles, deciles, percentiles. Two types: Less than ogive + More than ogive.

#### Circular / Pie Diagram
- Circle divided into sectors proportional to values
- 360° divided in proportion to magnitude of each item
- Best when components >3–4 (bar diagrams look crowded)

#### Pictogram
- Data represented by pictures — attractive but difficult to draw
- Not precise

#### Cartogram (Map Diagram)
- Data relating to geographical areas
- Represents: population density, crop yield, rainfall, bank deposits

## PART B: COLLECTION OF DATA

### 7. Primary vs Secondary Data
| Type | Definition | Reliability |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| **Primary** | Collected for first time for current objective | More reliable (purpose-specific) |
| **Secondary** | Used by others previously; already collected by another agency | Must be used carefully; cost of collection lower |

**3 Checks before using Secondary data:**
1. Are data suitable for current investigation?
2. Are data adequate?
3. Are data reliable?

---

### 8. Methods of Collecting Primary Data (5)

| Method | Best For | Key Feature |
|--------|---------|------------|
| **Direct Personal Observation** | Narrow scope, confidential, complex investigations | Most accurate; expensive; investigator bias possible |
| **Indirect Oral Interview** | Large area; reluctant respondents; confidential info | From 3rd party/witness; economical; may lack homogeneity |
| **Local Agencies / Correspondents** | Very large area; periodic collection | Used by newspapers; lacks originality |
| **Questionnaire (filled by respondent)** | Extensive area; educated respondents | Free from investigator bias; non-response problem |
| **Schedule (filled by investigator)** | Extensive area; illiterate respondents | Most accurate; very expensive; investigator bias |

> **Key difference — Questionnaire vs Schedule:** Same form; Questionnaire filled by respondent; Schedule filled by investigator.

---

### 9. Drafting a Questionnaire — Key Rules (12 rules, know these 6)

1. Questions: simple, unambiguous, precise
2. Minimum number of questions — only directly relevant ones
3. Avoid questions touching pride/sentiments or private affairs
4. Avoid mathematical computation questions
5. Logical sequence — natural, spontaneous replies
6. Test check (pilot testing) before actual use

**4 Types of Questions:**
- Simple alternative (Yes/No)
- Multiple choice (tick one)
- Specific information (How many? What income?)
- Open questions (respondent's own words)
