## 1. Profit — Definition & Types

**Profit** = positive gain from business operations after subtracting all expenses. Reward for entrepreneurial risk-bearing under uncertainty.

### Profit vs Returns — 3 Key Differences
| Difference | Profit | Returns (wages, interest) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Residual** income | Total revenue |
| Sign | Can be **negative** | Always positive |
| Volatility | **Greater fluctuations** | More stable |

### 3 Types of Profit

**A. Accounting Profit**
- Explicit costs only (cash payments to outsiders)
- **Formula:** Accounting Profit = TR − (W + R + I + M)
  - W = Wages & Salaries, R = Rent, I = Interest, M = Materials
- Used for taxable income calculation; also called **gross profit**
- Net profit = Gross profit − Depreciation − Taxes

**B. Economic Profit**
- Explicit + implicit (opportunity) costs
- **Formula:** Economic Profit = TR − (Explicit costs + Implicit costs)
- Implicit costs: rent on own land, owner's salary, interest on own capital
- Economic profit < 0 = economic loss → resources better used elsewhere

**C. Pure Profit**
- **Formula:** Pure Profit = Accounting Profit − (Opportunity Cost + Unauthorized payments like bribes)

## 2. Theories of Profit — 5

| Theory | Economist | Core Idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rent Theory** | F.A. Walker | Profit = differential ability of entrepreneur; like rent is differential fertility of land | Only measures profit; doesn't explain its nature; monopoly position also drives profit |
| **Dynamic Theory** | J.B. Clark | Profit arises only in dynamic economies (changing population, capital, wants, technology); static economy = only normal profit | Knight: only unforeseen changes yield profit; if change is predictable, no profit arises |
| **Risk Theory** | F.B. Hawley (1893) | Profit = reward for risk-taking; greater risk → higher expected profit | Profit doesn't always match risk; superior entrepreneurs reduce risk rather than just bearing it |
| **Uncertainty-Bearing Theory** | Frank Knight | Profit = reward for bearing **non-calculable (uninsurable) uncertainty**, NOT calculable (insurable) risk | Ignores coordination and market share as sources of profit |
| **Innovation Theory** | J.A. Schumpeter | Profit = reward for innovation (new products, methods, markets, raw materials) | Ignores uncertainty and risk as profit sources |

**Knight's key distinction:**
- **Calculable risks** — can estimate probability; can be insured (fire, theft) → borne by insurance firms, NOT entrepreneur
- **Non-calculable risks** (uncertainty) — shifts in demand, market changes → entrepreneur bears these → earns profit

## 3. Functions of Profit (Peter Drucker — 3)

1. **Tool for measuring performance** — high profit = efficient management
2. **Source of covering costs** — replacement, technical, uncertainty costs
3. **Aid to ensure future capital** — retained profits → investment, expansion, innovation

**Additional positive effects of high profit:**
- Investment in R&D → better technology
- Higher dividends → attracts investors
- Cushion against recession/inflation
- Higher taxes paid → government social welfare spending

## 4. Profit Maximisation

**Condition:** Two rules must be satisfied simultaneously:
1. **MC = MR**
2. **MC curve cuts MR curve from below** (ensures it's a maximum, not minimum)

**Formula:** Maximise π(Q) = R(Q) − C(Q)

### Under Perfect Competition
- MR curve = horizontal (price taker); MR = AR = Price
- Equilibrium: MC = MR = AR (Price)
- MC curve cuts MR from below at equilibrium

### Under Monopoly
- Downward sloping demand (AR) curve
- **MR < AR** at equilibrium
- Equilibrium: MC = MR < AR (Price)

### 9 Criticisms of Profit Maximisation Theory

1. **Profits uncertain** — can't know future revenues and costs with certainty
2. **No relevance to internal organisation** — managers focus on assets/sales growth, not just profit
3. **No perfect knowledge** — firms don't know demand curves; always operate under uncertainty
4. **Empirical evidence vague** — most firms don't rank profit as primary goal
5. **Firms don't use MC=MR** — Hall & Hitch: businessmen unaware of MR/MC concepts
6. **Average cost principle** — firms use price = AVC + AFC + profit margin (≈10%) in practice
7. **Static theory** — ignores temporal interdependence of decisions (past decisions affect future)
8. **Not applicable to oligopoly** — abandoned for oligopoly/duopoly due to interdependence
9. **Varied objectives** — modern corporations: managers separate from shareholders; pursue sales/growth/utility maximisation (Berle & Means, 1932)

## 5. Break-Even Analysis

**Definition:** Study of interrelationship between sales revenue, costs, and profit at different output levels. Point where TR = TC.

**Break-Even Point formula:**
> **BEP (units) = Fixed Cost / (Price − AVC)**
> = FC / Contribution Margin per unit

**Key Formulas:**

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Contribution Margin (CM) per unit | P − AVC |
| Total Contribution Margin (TCM) | TCM = TNP + TFC = TR − TVC |
| Profit-Volume (P/V) ratio | = CM / Price = (P − AVC) / P |
| BEP in rupee sales | = FC / P/V ratio |
| BEP as % of capacity | = BEP units / Capacity |

**3 Ways to compute BEP:**
1. Number of units to sell
2. Rupee value of sales
3. Percentage of plant capacity

### Indian Airlines Example (from ebook)
- Capacity: 10,000 passengers/month; Fare: ₹500; AVC: ₹100; FC: ₹3,00,000/month
- CM = ₹500 − ₹100 = **₹400**
- BEP = ₹3,00,000 / ₹400 = **750 passengers** to break even

### Applications of Break-Even Analysis
1. What happens to profitability when a new product is introduced?
2. What sales level is needed to earn a target profit?
3. Effect of price change on revenues and costs?
4. Impact of new equipment or higher fixed costs?
5. Which of two investments offers greater profit margin?
6. Revenue and cost implications of changing production process
7. Make vs buy vs lease capital equipment decision

### Product Mix Optimisation (Limiting Factor)
- When capacity is a constraint, prioritise product with highest **contribution per facility/machine hour**
- Example: Product X with ₹250 contribution/hour preferred over Y with ₹200 contribution/hour

### Key relationships:
- At BEP: ANP = 0, therefore **AFC = ACM** (Average Contribution Margin = Average Fixed Cost)
- SR: Cover variable costs first; if P > AVC, continue to expand to cover fixed costs

## 6. Inflation

**Definition:** Quantitative measure of rate at which average price level increases over time. = Decrease in purchasing power of currency.

### 3 Causes / Types of Inflation

| Type | Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand-Pull** | Excess demand over production capacity | Demand > Supply → prices rise; also: money supply increase → more spending → inflation |
| **Cost-Push** | Rise in production input costs | Higher wages/raw materials → higher finished product prices |
| **Built-In (Wage-Price Spiral)** | Adaptive expectations | Prices rise → workers demand higher wages → costs rise → prices rise further (self-perpetuating) |

### Inflation Measurement Indexes

| Index | Measures | Used By |
|---|---|---|
| **CPI (Consumer Price Index)** | Weighted average of consumer goods basket (transport, food, medical) — retail prices | Most countries; US Bureau of Labor Statistics (monthly since 1913) |
| **WPI (Wholesale Price Index)** | Prices at producer/wholesale level; before retail | India and many countries |
| **PPI (Producer Price Index)** | Average change in selling prices received by domestic producers | USA (variant of WPI) |

### Inflation Formula
> **Change in Inflation = Final CPI / Initial CPI**

**Ebook numerical example:**
- Sept 1975 CPI = 54.6 → Sept 2018 CPI = 252.439
- Rise in inflation = 252.439 / 54.6 = **4.6234 (462.34%)**
- $10,000 in 1975 = $10,000 × 4.6234 + $10,000 = **$56,234.25** in 2018

### Effects of Inflation

**A. On Income Distribution:**
| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Debtors/Borrowers** | GAIN — repay in cheaper rupees |
| **Creditors/Lenders** | LOSE — receive cheaper rupees |
| **Bond/Debenture holders** | LOSE — fixed interest income erodes |
| **Investors (equity)** | GAIN — stock prices rise with inflation |
| **Fixed income earners (salaried)** | LOSE — wage increases lag price rises |
| **Profit-earners/Speculators** | GAIN — flexible incomes rise faster |

**B. On Economic Growth:**
- Mild inflation: **favourable** — incentivises investment, raises output (multiplier effect)
- High/Hyperinflation: **harmful** — uncertainty, lower investment, capital flight, lower savings → lower capital formation
- **Hyperinflation** = ≥50% inflation per month (e.g., Germany 1920s — 1.3 billion × price rise in 1923)

### Controlling Inflation
- **Monetary policy** — central bank controls money supply and interest rates
- Target inflation rates: USA ≈ 2%; India ≈ 4%; Brazil ≈ 4.25%
- Tools: interest rate hikes, quantitative tightening

### Hedging Against Inflation (Investor Angle)
1. **Stocks** — best hedge; price rises pass through to profits and stock prices
2. **TIPS** (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) — principal indexed to inflation
3. **Gold** — traditional hedge (though not always reliable)
4. **REITs** — real estate investment trusts
5. **Commodities** — tangible assets rise with inflation

> 🫀 *Trading analogy: Inflation erodes fixed income (bonds) but benefits equity holders — just like in portfolio management, you tilt to equities during inflationary cycles. TIPS = RBI floating rate bonds for Indian investors.*
