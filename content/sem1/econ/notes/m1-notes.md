## 1. Definition

> **Managerial Economics** = Application of economic principles & methodologies to business decision-making within a firm.

| Scholar | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| **Mansfield** | Applying economic concepts to rational decision-making |
| **Spencer** | Integration of economic theory with business practice |
| **Douglas** | Rules & principles to attain economic goals of management |
| **Haynes et al.** | Economic tools relevant to firm's decision-making process |

**Key idea:** Bridge between Economics (resource allocation) and Management (decision-making).

> 🫀 *Clinical analogy: Like evidence-based medicine — applying research principles (economics) to bedside decisions (management).*

## 3. Types of Managerial Economics

- **Liberal** — Follow market and customer demand; democratic, market-driven
- **Normative** — Decisions based on real-world experience; practical (demand forecasting, cost management)
- **Radical** — Focus on changing the status quo; customer satisfaction over profit maximization

## 4. Mankiw's 10 Principles

### How People Make Decisions (4)
1. **Tradeoffs** — every choice sacrifices something else
2. **Opportunity Cost** — cost of the next best alternative foregone
3. **Marginalism** — rational people think at the margin (incremental gain/cost)
4. **Incentives** — people respond to positive/negative incentives

### How People Interact (3)
5. **Trade benefits everyone** — comparative advantage drives exchange
6. **Markets organise activity** — price signals coordinate supply & demand
7. **Government can improve outcomes** — intervenes for welfare, minimum wages, externalities

### How the Economy Works (3)
8. **Standard of living = productivity** — output per worker drives GDP
9. **Money supply → Inflation** — excess money → excess demand → rising prices
10. **Inflation-Unemployment tradeoff** — short-run Phillips Curve

## 5. Characteristics of Managerial Economics

- Studies individual firm / industry (micro focus)
- Pragmatic — ignores overly theoretical assumptions
- Normative and applied — prescribes action, not just describes
- Incorporates macroeconomic context (business cycles, tax policy)
- Scientific art — optimum resource use under uncertainty
- Aids goal determination, planning, and decision-making

## 7. Objectives of the Firm

### A. Profit Maximisation (Traditional)
- **First-order condition:** MR = MC
- **Second-order condition:** MC cuts MR from below (MC rising, MR falling)
- Mathematically: second derivative of profit function < 0

### B. Baumol's Sales Revenue Maximisation
- **Goal:** Maximise total revenue (not volume), subject to **minimum profit constraint**
- Why managers prefer sales over profit:
  - Manager's salary/status tied to sales, not profit
  - Banks assess firms by sales growth
  - Growing sales resolves HR problems
- **Without advertising:** Sales-max output (OD) > Profit-max output (OQ); price lower
- **With advertising:** Sales-maximiser spends MORE on advertising than profit-maximiser
- **Criticisms:** Ignores oligopolistic interdependence; no industry equilibrium shown

> 🫀 *Trading analogy: Like a broker maximising AUM (assets under management) subject to a minimum revenue constraint — not always the same as maximising their own profit.*

### C. Marris's Growth Maximisation
- **Goal:** Maximise balanced growth rate G = Gd (demand growth) = Gc (capital growth)
- Subject to: Managerial constraint + Financial constraint
- Manager's utility and owner's utility both grow with firm size

### D. Williamson's Managerial Utility Maximisation
- **Goal:** Managers maximise their own utility function: **U = f(S, M, D)**
  - **S** = Staff expenditure (salary + subordinates)
  - **M** = Managerial slack (perks, cars, expense accounts)
  - **D** = Discretionary investment (funds manager invests at will)
- Profit types: Actual → Reported → Minimum required → Discretionary
- Formula: **D = πR − π0 − T** (discretionary profit = reported profit − minimum required − tax)
- **Criticisms:** Doesn't explain oligopoly behaviour; insufficient empirical evidence

### E. Cyert & March — Behavioural Theory
- Modern firm = coalition of stakeholders with **multiple, conflicting goals**
- **5 Goals:**
  1. Production goal
  2. Inventory goal
  3. Sales goal
  4. Market-share goal
  5. Profit goal
- **Satisficing behaviour** — aspiration levels revised up (if exceeded) or down (if not met); not maximising
- **Organisational slack** = excess payments to coalition members above minimum needed; acts as cushion during downturns
- **Decision-making:** Top management sets goals + allocates budget; lower levels implement
- **Criticisms:** Specific to duopoly; ignores entry threat; only short-run focused

| Model | Primary Goal | Key Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Profit Max | Max π | MR = MC |
| Baumol | Max Sales Revenue | Min profit |
| Marris | Max Growth | Managerial + Financial |
| Williamson | Max Manager Utility (S, M, D) | Min shareholder profit |
| Cyert & March | Satisfice multiple goals | Organisational slack |

## 8. Fundamental Economic Concepts

### A. Incremental Concept
- **Decision is profitable if:** incremental revenue > incremental cost
- 4 conditions: ΔR > ΔC; reduces some cost more than increases others; increases some revenues more than it decreases others; reduces costs more than revenues
- **Key:** Ignore fixed/sunk costs; only variable incremental costs matter
- **Limitation:** Only valid with excess capacity; short-run only

> *Example: Accept an order at below full-cost if idle capacity exists and incremental cost (₹7,000) < incremental revenue (₹10,000)*

### B. Time Perspective Concept
- Balance **short-run** and **long-run** effects of decisions
- Short run → output changes without changing firm size (variable factors only)
- Long run → firm changes size (all factors variable)
- SR: AC may differ from AR. LR: AC = AR (equilibrium)
- Risk: Accepting low-price orders short-term may damage brand + pricing power long-term

### C. Opportunity Cost Concept
- = Revenue/benefit foregone from the next best alternative
- Not recorded in books of accounts (notional cost)
- If resource has no alternative use → opportunity cost = 0
- Uses: relative pricing of goods; factor remuneration; optimal resource allocation

### D. Discounting Principle (Time Value of Money)
- A rupee today > a rupee tomorrow (can earn return in the interim)
- **Formula:** V = A / (1 + i)^n
  - V = Present Value
  - A = Future Amount
  - i = Interest/discount rate
  - n = Number of years
- *Example: ₹100 at 5% for 1 year → V = 100/1.05 = ₹95.24*
- Most useful in: capital budgeting, investment planning

> 🫀 *Trading analogy: Exactly how you value future cash flows in DCF analysis — core to equity valuation.*

### E. Equimarginal Principle
- Allocate resources such that **Value of Marginal Product (VMP) is equal across all uses**
- Formula: **VMPLA = VMPLB = VMPLC = VMPLD = VMPLE**
- If VMP in Activity A < VMP in Activity B → shift resources from A to B
- Applies to: budget allocation, investment decisions, consumer utility maximisation

> 🫀 *Clinical analogy: Like allocating cath lab time — where marginal benefit per case (primary PCI vs elective PCI) is equalised across patient priority groups.*
